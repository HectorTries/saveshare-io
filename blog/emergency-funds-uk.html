The safety net every creator mentions before investing a penny

← Blog Saving

No story on this page starts with investing. Every one of them starts with a buffer, a boring pile of cash sitting exactly where it can be reached, waiting for the boiler to break or the contract to end. The creators disagree about plenty, funds and pensions and property, but on this they speak with one voice. Before a single pound goes near the stock market, three to six months of essential spending must sit somewhere safe. What differs is how they learned it, how much they keep, and what the money bought them beyond safety.

The sharpest version comes from Mia Rose, whose video on financially preparing to move out has been watched more than 58,000 times. She lays down the rule before anything about rent or bills, and she frames it as the price of independence itself.

Mia Rose

Mia Rose@miarosemcgrath

If you are living at home and working, I would be aiming to save at least a three month emergency fund before you decide to actually move out. An emergency fund is there in case you have an emergency, like your boiler breaks or you lose your job, and you need money to cover you while you sort it out.

Watch on TikTok (58,472 views, 5,669 likes). Her six step move out guide has 296 shares.

Mia built a multiple six figure net worth in her twenties, with 100,000 pounds invested, and she treats the emergency fund as choreography rather than caution. Save first, move second, and the freedom that follows is real because it was paid for in advance. The fund is not pessimism. It is the entry ticket to every bigger decision, and she refuses to let her viewers buy the ticket after they have already walked through the door.

JNO arrives at the same rule from the opposite direction, as step two of a deliberate pre investing checklist. In a video laying out the three things he did before putting a single pound into the stock market, clearing expensive debt comes first and the buffer comes immediately after, sized with unusual precision.

JNO

JNO@jno_pwnr

The first thing I did was get rid of any high interest debt, so things like credit cards, because if you are paying 20 percent interest on a credit card, you would be much better off paying that off instead of investing in something that is going to go up or down. The second thing I did was build an emergency fund. For me, I decided to save six months of expenses so that if anything happened, I would be covered.

Watch on TikTok (1,592 views). Part one of his Investing from Zero series.

Six months rather than three, and the reasoning is pure arithmetic. A credit card charging 20 percent will devour any market return, so debt dies first. Then the fund gets built to six months of expenses, not income, a distinction the careful creators always make. JNO counts what leaving costs, not what arriving pays, and the fund covers exactly that number for exactly half a year.

Ciara gives the idea its most methodical expression. In a video watched more than 33,000 times, she describes going from zero to 100,000 pounds invested in just under five years, and she names the emergency fund as the first of three things she wishes she had known, with a calculation method attached.

Ciara

Ciara@ciara__anne

You need to be saving three to six months emergency fund. I know this is a lot of money and it does not sound very fun, but you need to do it. You first need to calculate your essential expenses, so think rent, mortgage, bills, things that you absolutely have to pay, and then multiply that by three or by six.

Watch on TikTok (33,418 views). Her most watched money video, and the one her viewers quote back at her.

Ciara is blunt that the number feels miserable. Nobody dreams about holding six months of rent in cash while markets rise without them. But she went from being, in her own words, pretty dumb with money to holding multiple six figures invested, and the unglamorous fund was the foundation she refused to skip. Essentials only, multiplied by three or six, held in cash, and only then does the investing conversation begin.

Chris shows what the finished product looks like in the wild. He holds 5,444 pounds in savings, every pound assigned a purpose, and he walks his viewers through the architecture in a video that draws thousands of views from savers who recognise their own chaos in his before picture.

Chris

Chris@intentional.finance

I have 5,444 pounds in savings currently, and I have set up my accounts in a way that means every pound has a purpose. I am building an emergency fund of three to six months of my essential living expenses to ensure that I am protected should anything go wrong, whether that be losing my job, getting ill or anything like that.

Watch on TikTok (8,047 views). He also saves monthly for annual bills like car insurance, to dodge monthly payment interest.

Chris bought a flat at 21 with a 30,000 pound deposit, skipped university, and job hopped his way from a 19,000 pound starting salary upward, so his respect for buffers is earned rather than theoretical. His system gives each pound a job, the emergency fund guards against catastrophe, the bills pot smooths the lumpy costs, and nothing sits anywhere by accident. It is the least exciting video on this page and possibly the most useful.

Then Amy renames the whole concept and reveals what it is really for. Her fund is not called an emergency fund at all. It is a fund with a ruder name and a grander purpose, and it bought her something no emergency ever could, the ability to walk away from a six figure banking job with nothing lined up.

Amy

Amy@financiallyher_

I quit my six figure job in banking without another job lined up and to do this, I spent years building a fund that buys freedom. Most people might call it an emergency fund, but I think that is a little bit too limiting for what it can do for you. Because that money is not just for emergencies. It is the money to buy you the ability to leave, whether that be a job, a relationship, or just take three months to figure out what is next.

Watch on TikTok (1,387 views). She built six figures invested by 26, mostly by raising her income rather than cutting coffee.

Where they agree, and where they split, is the question of size and purpose, and the fault lines are honest ones. Mia says three months is enough to move out on. JNO wants six months before investing a penny. Ciara offers the range and makes viewers do the multiplication themselves. Chris holds 5,444 pounds and keeps building toward the top of the range. Amy keeps the largest buffer of all, years of freedom money, because her fund insures not her boiler but her autonomy. Nobody on this page says skip it. Nobody says one month will do. The argument is only ever about whether the fund protects a life or purchases one, and the wisest answer in the room is that it does both in that order.

The practical synthesis fits in a single breath. Add up the spending you cannot cancel, rent or mortgage plus bills plus food plus transport, multiply by three at minimum and six for comfort, park it in an instant access account you never otherwise touch, and only then open the investing app. Every creator here did it in that order, from the 25 year old with six figures to the banker who bought her own resignation. The market will still be there when the buffer is built. The emergencies, as several of them cheerfully note, will not wait, which is exactly why the buffer comes first.

Mia adds two rehearsals that turn the fund from a number into a habit. In the same move out guide she tells viewers to save the average rent for their region every single month before moving, and for Greater London she names the figure precisely.

Mia Rose@miarosemcgrath

I would be aiming to save every single month the average amount of rent for the particular region that you are going to move to. For Greater London, this is 915 pounds. And I did this because psychologically, it got me used to not having that money there to spend.

Watch on TikTok (58,472 views, 5,669 likes). She also urges a second income stream, saying she never wants to be reliant on one source of income, especially in this economy.
