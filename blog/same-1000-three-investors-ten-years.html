Same Stake, Three Investors, Ten Years

One keeps cash, one picks stocks, one buys the boring index fund and leaves it alone

By SaveShare.io · 5 November 2026

← Blog Investing Compounding

Amanda stands beside a simple chart and asks what happens to £1,000 or $1,000 left alone for ten years. She is an investor who hit seven figures by 27, self taught and the daughter of immigrants, and she has a gift for making arithmetic feel like drama. Her setup is disarmingly plain: the same £1,000 handed to three investors, ten years on the clock, and three wildly different endings.

The first investor puts the same £1,000 into a regular savings account paying 0.5% interest, and ten years later holds $1,051, basically nothing after inflation. The second buys the S&P 500, which Amanda notes has averaged over 10% a year across the last 50 years, and finishes with $2,594, money doubled while they slept. The third buys the same index but adds $100 every month, and ends with over $22,000. Same starting sum, same decade, entirely different endings.

The uncomfortable question is why so few people become the third investor when the recipe fits on an index card. The creators gathered here keep returning to the same answer: investing is behaviour wearing a maths costume, a test of boredom tolerance and nerve more than of cleverness. They argue about which investments deserve the money, but they speak as one about waiting, fiddling, and quitting early.

Nobody sets the scene better than Amanda, who posted the three investor comparison to Instagram, and whose video on the end of 5% mortgages drew 42,314 views. She opens with the saver, and delivers the bad news first.

Amanda

Amanda@amandainvests

Let's say we have three investors, right? One person is putting $1,000 into a regular savings account. Let's say 0.5% interest. At the end of 10 years, they have $1,051, basically nothing after inflation.

Watch on Instagram (Instagram clip comparing all three outcomes side by side).

From there she walks through the index buyer and the monthly contributor, and lands the moral without mercy. The biggest mistake, she says, is waiting: every year spent insisting the moment has passed is a year of compound growth that never comes back. It is a bracing message from someone who describes herself as self taught, and it reframes the saver not as prudent but as quietly expensive. Cash feels safe because its number never falls, yet the third investor lets time do the heavy lifting instead. Amanda keeps a sleeve of her own money in individual company shares, a wrinkle worth remembering when the index loyalists have their say below.

If Amanda prices a decade of waiting, Krish prices a fortnight of it. In a video watched 34,800 times, he argues that the most expensive sentence in investing is the decision that markets have risen too far to join. His evidence is a single sum left alone for ten years, and what happens when its owner tries to be clever about timing.

Krish

Krish@krish.kara

The most expensive word in investing is it's too high. £10,000 fully invested in the SMP 500 10 years ago is worth £35,000 today.

Watch on TikTok (34,800 views, 62 likes). On why waiting for the perfect moment backfires.

The rest of his video fills in the damage. Miss just the ten best days and the £10,000 grows to only £18,500, nearly half the gains gone from skipping 0.4% of trading days. Worse, eight of the ten best days arrive within two weeks of the ten worst days, which means the days everyone wants to dodge and the days nobody can afford to miss come as a pair. It is the statistical backbone of Amanda's third investor: regular money in, no clever exits, and the calendar left to work.

Neil came to the same conclusion by a longer road. At 37 he decided he wanted out of corporate life by 50, a feat he notes only 1 to 2 percent of people in the UK manage, and he picked long term investing as the vehicle. He tells the story in a video watched 16,611 times, after 20 years in meetings he never wants to repeat.

Neil

Neil@neilinvests

The way that I worked out the best way of doing that was gonna be around investing. I'm not talking about stock picking, I'm not talking about meme coins. I'm talking about long term investing, which has been proven for decades to be one of the best ways for building long term wealth.

Watch on TikTok (16,611 views, 294 likes). On choosing early retirement at 37.

Neil dates his decision to January the 1st of 2020, and he has the receipts to show for it. He now holds £350,000 across his investment portfolios, and he explains that at a 4% yearly withdrawal the pot should last indefinitely, because markets have grown by 9% on average across decades. On his balance that means living on £14,000 a year, which he admits would be an extremely frugal lifestyle, so he keeps building rather than coasting.

Theory is one thing and a bank statement is another, which is where Sophie comes in. Her most watched video, a tour of London wealth from Notting Hill to Mayfair, drew 75,549 views and 5,140 likes. In the summer of 2018 she put £100 into her first investment knowing nothing, and she has tracked the compounding ever since.

Sophie

Sophie@itssophieblank

I made my first investment of £100 in the summer of 2018. And this is how my net worth has developed every single year. In 2018, I started working in my full time job, initially at a charity.

The numbers that follow are the third investor made flesh. Minus £28,000 in 2018, £10,500 by 2020, £50,000 in 2021, a dip to £43,000 after a sabbatical in 2022, then £105,000 in assets in 2023, £134,800 in 2024 once the student loan was paid off in full, £175,000 in 2025 on a newly six figure income, and £223,000 so far in 2026, aiming at a quarter of a million by year end. Her own caption to the video says simple ETFs bought every month are the way forward for her, and the balance history backs her up.

For anyone who finds those sums intimidating, JNO lowers the bar to the floor. His video on his income streams as a self employed 28 year old in London was watched 35,800 times. He started investing four years ago, and his counsel to newcomers is to begin embarrassingly small.

JNO

JNO@jno_pwnr

And the third thing I did was to start very small. When I started investing, I was putting in maybe 50 to 100 pounds a month. And honestly, it doesn't really matter how much you're putting in.

Watch on TikTok (1,592 views, 86 likes). Part one of his Investing from Zero series.

He means it literally: 5, 10 or 20 pounds a month all count, because what matters at the start is building the habit of moving a little money toward the market until the fear fades. Before any of that he cleared expensive debt, the kind charging 20% on credit cards, and built six months of expenses, about £15,000 at the time, in a high interest savings account. The buffer means a lost job never forces a sale at a loss to pay the rent. Start tiny, automate it, and raise the amounts only once confidence arrives.

The finished article, the third investor after years of practice, looks a lot like Mark. In a video watched 24,427 times he gives an update on almost £114,000 he put into Vanguard's new VALL fund, which tracks the global all cap index. A month in, the position is down, and he films the red number anyway.

Mark

Mark@markonthemoney

About a month ago now I invested almost £114,000 into Vanguard's brand new ETF, the V A L L, which tracks the global all world all cap index. [...] It's tracking an index that's existed for a very, very long time.

Watch on TikTok (24,427 views, 240 likes). On buying the boring global index.

The loss does not ruffle him because the comparison flatters the choice: his old fund would have fallen 1.38% over the same stretch, so the broader spread is working in his favour. On payday he pays himself first with £300 into his stocks and shares ISA, while £1,142 a month flows into his pension and the rest is budgeted to zero. This is the third investor as a monthly routine rather than a revelation, unglamorous and entirely repeatable.

Where they agree, and where they split

Neil, Krish and Mark sit squarely in one camp, with Sophie and JNO beside them. They agree that stock picking is a distraction for ordinary investors, that time in the market beats timing it, and that automation beats willpower.

Eight of the 10 best days happen within two weeks of the 10 worst days. It shows how difficult it is to consistently time the market.

Krish (watch the video)

The dissent comes from Amanda, and it is narrower than it first sounds. She would still put the bulk of a starter pot into two broad ETFs, then reserve a sleeve for shares in household names she believes in, building what she calls conviction in specific companies.

The last $400 is going to go to blue chip individual stock. You can spread these across three or four household names that you truly believe in. This is where you start building conviction in specific companies rather than the market as a whole.

Amanda (watch the video)

The weight of the room still sits with the index loyalists, and even the dissenter automates and starts with funds. Nobody on this page defends the savings account, nobody claims timing skill, and nobody pretends the early years feel exciting.

Open the account this week, automate a monthly payment you will not miss, and buy the broad index fund before you feel ready. Expect the early years to look boring, because compounding does its loudest work late, and the third investor is simply the first investor who refused to stop. Keep some cash for emergencies, but do not confuse it with a plan. Ten years from now the difference will not be who chose brilliantly; it will be who started, and who stayed.
