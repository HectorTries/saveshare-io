Side Hustle to Seven Figures: Trell, Tiffany and the Creators Who Turned Extra Income Into Wealth

Six months off everything, a crochet hook, a 70 percent savings rate and owned digital products, how extra income becomes real wealth

By SaveShare.io · 14 October 2026

Share this Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest

Side hustles, funding mechanisms for the investments that actually free you

Back to Blog Side hustles US

Trell told his audience to disappear. Six months off from everything, dating, birthdays, trips, six months of stacking every dollar, and you would be set for life. The video has been watched 335,000 times and liked 62,000 times by people who almost certainly will not do what it says. That is his whole point, and why it is the most watched side hustle video in this collection.

The tension underneath every story on this page is the same, and it is not really about which hustle pays best. It is about what the extra income is for. Everyone here agrees a second income stream matters, but they split hard on the next step. One camp says the hustle money should vanish straight into investments before lifestyle can touch it. Another says the hustle itself should become the business, something owned, something that pays while you sleep. And one voice warns that most creator income is rented from platforms that can change the rent whenever they like. Same starting line, very different finish lines.

Trell the Trainer is the anchor of this page because his numbers are the loudest and his prescription the most extreme. A former trainer who put his first $7,000 of earnings into the stock market and watched it approach half a million dollars, he credits nothing to stock picking and everything to rate, putting 70 percent of his income into the market consistently for six to seven years. His five tips video, the 335,000 view one, moves from sacrifice to generosity to investing to pricing to storytelling in under three minutes, and every step is really about the same thing, refusing to consume.

Trell the Trainer

Trell the Trainer@trellthetrainer

First thing is you are gonna have to take six months off. Six months off from everything. Dating, birthdays, trips. Six months. That is all it takes. Think about how many people can not do that. Imagine if you go missing for six months and you stack up everything. You will be set for the rest of your life.

Watch on TikTok (335,040 views, 62,760 likes). His top five tips for getting rich, led by six months of total sacrifice.

The second tip reframes the hustle entirely. Give everything away for free, build trust first, sell later. All of his content is free, and he says he still made $2 million, because free value creates an audience dying to pay for the rest. His $7,000 of trainer earnings, invested and compounded at a 70 percent savings rate over six to seven years, now approaches half a million dollars. His daily habits video, watched nearly 72,000 times, fills in the machinery. Same clothes, home cooking, daily account checks, and 80 percent of working time on content creation. He saved his first $10,000 eating at home. The frugality was never the identity, it was the launchpad.

Trell the Trainer@trellthetrainer

I spend 80 percent of my working time on the one skill that has made me the most money. For me, that is content creation. You can not manage your money if you refuse to track your money. I know what came in, I know what went out, and I know what I invested and what I spent.

Watch on TikTok (71,697 views, 9,766 likes). Six daily habits, with content creation getting 80 percent of working time.

Tiffany Morrison films from the opposite end of the journey, the beginning, on camera, without knowing the ending. At 40, after nearly 20 years working full time, she suddenly lost her paycheck, and instead of waiting to be hired she bet on the corner of the internet she had spent years building. The goal is not just to replace her old $60,000 salary with one income stream but to build several, through content, brand partnerships, and now products of her own. She picked up her crochet hook and asked her audience to come along.

Tiffany Morrison

Tiffany Morrison@singlemombudgeting

Hi, I am Tiffany. I am a single mom. I just turned 40, and after nearly 20 years of working full time, I suddenly found myself without a paycheck. And while I am looking for another job, I decided I am not about to sit here and wait around for someone to hire me.

Watch on TikTok. A newly jobless mom turns content and crochet into multiple income streams.

What makes Tiffany's story belong here is the honesty about scale. Crocheting alone will probably not replace a $60,000 salary, and she says so herself. Where Trell preaches the six month sprint from a position of arrival, Tiffany documents the first steps from inside the uncertainty.

Tiffany Morrison@singlemombudgeting

The goal is not just to replace my old 60,000 dollar salary with only one income stream. Instead, I am trying to build multiple streams of income through content creation, brand partnerships, and now products of my own.

Watch on TikTok. Multiple streams, not one replacement paycheck, is the stated goal.

Neelima offers the most structured hustle to wealth pipeline, backed by seven figures built in six years from knowing nothing about money. Her three moves: a top one percent salary for your role, investing tools so the paycheck compounds, and trying things until something works.

If you told me I would become a millionaire in my 20s, when I started my first job six years ago, I would have told you you are absolutely insane, because I knew nothing about money when I first graduated. I had no idea what a 401k was, no idea how the stock market worked, and I kept my entire net worth in cash because I was so scared of investing.

Watch on TikTok (2,497 views, 127 likes). From cash under the mattress to seven figures in six years.

Neelima's framing reorders the usual hustle advice. Most side hustle content starts with the extra gig, but she starts with the main gig, arguing that a top percentile salary turns every subsequent habit into a multiplier. The side experiments come third, after earning power and investing knowledge, and her permission to fail at them is explicit. It is Trell's intensity with the volume lowered and the sequencing made legible.

Mia Rose McGrath gives the side hustle its most practical defense, security rather than riches. In a video watched 58,000 times, she explains why the second stream mattered. Her 9 to 5 in fashion was not enough security alone, so she built content creation alongside it.

Mia Rose McGrath

Mia Rose McGrath@miarosemcgrath

Having a side hustle really helped me. I do not ever want to be reliant on one source of income, especially in this economy. And so when I was employed at my 9 to 5 in fashion, I just felt like that was not enough security for me. I had to have an additional stream of income, which for me was content creation.

Watch on TikTok (58,472 views, 5,669 likes). The side hustle as security, not lottery ticket.

Mia also concedes what the hustle bros rarely do, that not everyone has the time or energy to start one. Her advice survives contact with exhaustion because it is framed as insurance you buy if you can, not a moral test you fail if you cannot.

Bret and Evey bring the warning this page needs, delivered with charts. Citing major creator economy reports, they show that around 70 percent of creator revenue comes from brand deals and platform ad shares, income where you control neither the budget nor the algorithm, and that less than 4 percent of creators make a full time living strictly from views. Their video is small, a few hundred views, but the argument is the load bearing one. If your side hustle is content, renting your income from platforms is not a business, and the fix is shifting earnings into owned assets like digital products and merchandise.

Bret and Evey

Around 70 percent of creator revenue comes from brand deals and platform ad shares. The big problem with the 70 percent being brand deals and ad revenue is you do not control the budget, you do not control the algorithm. And that is why less than 4 percent of creators make a full time living strictly from views.

Watch on TikTok. The rented income problem, with the Goldman Sachs numbers.

Their prescription is to build a personal brand that sells its own products, so content becomes marketing for something owned rather than labor for someone else's algorithm. Tiffany, building brand partnerships as one of several streams, lives inside exactly the dependency they describe, which is presumably why she is diversifying into products of her own.

Bret and Evey@bretandevey

Smart creators are shifting more of those bricks into owned assets like digital products, apparel or even physical merchandise as well. So I do not think the solution is to work harder making more content for the algorithm payouts, but to be building your own personal brand, your own products, which in turn will turn into a real business.

Watch on TikTok. Owned products, not harder grinding, is the way out of rented income.

Where they agree, and where they split

On the core claim there is no daylight. Every creator here treats a second income stream as necessary, not optional, and every one of them directs it somewhere more ambitious than spending. Trell invests 70 percent. Neelima compounds a top salary with market returns. Mia buys security against single income fragility. Tiffany builds several streams to replace one lost paycheck. Bret and Evey convert rented attention into owned assets. The unanimous vote is that one income is a risk and extra income is the hedge, whatever form the hedge takes.

The split opens on what the hustle money should become. Trell and Neelima sit firmly in the invest it camp, with Trell's $7,000 to nearly half a million dollars as the exhibit and Neelima's seven figures in six years as the second witness. Their argument is that labor scales badly and capital scales well, so the hustle exists to fund the portfolio. Bret and Evey push the other way, arguing the hustle should become an owned business, digital products and merchandise rather than index funds, because platform income can vanish while equity endures. Mia and Tiffany sit between the camps without quite joining either, Mia valuing the stream itself as insurance and Tiffany still building hers stream by stream, crochet hook in hand.

Nobody here says the hustle is the destination. It is the funding round, the insurance policy, or the raw material for something owned. If all five would sign one sentence, it is Trell's. You can not manage your money if you refuse to track your money.

The practical close is deliberately unspectacular. Pick one extra stream you can sustain for six months without burning out, a skill you already have, a product you can make, content about something you know. Direct the first fruits somewhere they compound, investments, a deposit fund, an owned product, rather than lifestyle. Track every dollar in and out the way Trell does, because the stream you do not measure leaks. Tiffany is still at the beginning, Trell near half a million from $7,000, and the distance between them is mostly time, attention, and the refusal to spend what the hustle earns.
