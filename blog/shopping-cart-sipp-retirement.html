Melissa is 38, on track to retire with $3.3 million by her own maths, and out of patience with anyone overcomplicating the process. Her twenty minute setup video has been watched 922 times with 176 likes, 16 comments and 18 shares, and it plays less like advice than an ultimatum. Start investing, start today, get it, got it, good. Its engagement rate of 0.31 is the highest on this page, which suggests the small audience that found it forwarded it to friends who needed the scolding.

The tragedy underneath every voice here is administrative. Opening a retirement account feels like the achievement, so thousands open one, transfer money in, and never buy anything. The cash idles while its owner tells friends the retirement is sorted. Pensions feel decades away, the paperwork looks dull, and the jargon does its best to repel beginners. Every creator here agrees the fix is mechanical. Open the account, then invest what is inside it.

Steve Chen is the cautionary tale that proves her point, a man who did everything right except the one step that mattered. He opened a Roth at a big brokerage, deposited monthly, and assumed the balance would grow alone. Viewers pass his confession around as the classic unfunded cart warning.

Steve Chen

Steve Chen@calltoleap

So the biggest mistake I made when it came to investing was when I went to my brokerage like Fidelity and Schwab and clicked on open an account, opened a Roth IRA and deposited money into my account every month. I thought my money was automatically going to grow. But I later found out that I was supposed to buy assets that grow over time like low cost index funds.

Watch on TikTok (the confession viewers share as the classic unfunded cart warning). Contributing is not investing.

His maths turns the mistake into a three million dollar gap. Max out the Roth yearly and invest it properly, and forty years of average returns lands near $3.3 million. Contribute the same money but never invest it and the total is around $300,000. Same deposits, same account, different retirements.

And if I max out my Roth IRA every year and did this over the next 40 years, I would have around $3.3 million with average returns. But if I never invested my money, I would only have $300,000.

Steve Chen, @calltoleap

Where they agree, and where they split

On mechanics there is total agreement across all six. Open the account early, fund it, and invest what is inside rather than letting cash idle. Melissa orders it, Steve proves it with his $3.3 million versus $300,000 gap, Josue makes it rules three and five of adult life, Jessica explains nobody else will do it since pensions died, Daniel sets the British target at twice salary by forty, and Rex films himself doing it with £17,600 of evidence.

The split opens on intensity and stock selection. Josue dissents from the gentle camp with finance maxing urgency, max the Roth in your twenties or struggle for decades, while Daniel answers from the measured pole with benchmarks that refuse to judge anyone behind. Rex breaks from the index consensus entirely, running a concentrated three stock SIPP while Melissa and Steve preach total market funds, a stance paying off so far with Meta up 17 percent but carrying exactly the single stock risk the grocery metaphor warns against.

Open one retirement account this week, fund it with whatever is available even if that means $5, and buy an investment inside it the same day so the cart is never empty. Take the full employer match if one exists, set a pension pot target if based in the UK, and automate the contribution so willpower can retire early. The pension era ended decades ago, so the shopping is now personal.
