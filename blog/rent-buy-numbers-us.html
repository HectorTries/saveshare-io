Rent, Buy, or Wait: Six Creators on the Numbers Behind the Front Door

Humphrey's 35% rent rule, Erika's $10 a day mortgage trick, Graham's cooling market, and the 815 credit score built at 15

By SaveShare.io · 5 November 2026

Share this Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest

The rule most renters carry in their heads is wrong, and the man correcting it does so with the patience of someone who has watched thousands of people misprice the biggest line in their budget. Thirty percent of gross salary, he says, is the wrong fraction of the wrong number, because nobody pays rent out of gross salary. Rent leaves the account after tax, so the only honest denominator is take home pay, and his replacement rule runs higher and stricter than the folk wisdom it retires.

The tension underneath runs through every voice on this page. Rent or buy, minimums or overpayments, loyalty or rate chasing, and each creator answers from a different seat at the table, the analyst, the lawyer, the agent, the teenager with perfect credit, the bargain hunter, the new homeowner furnishing empty rooms on borrowed money. What follows is the full set of numbers, and where their authors disagree.

Humphrey Yang opens with the affordability reframe, and his rent video drew 346,410 views with 11,862 likes and 271 comments, the kind of engagement that suggests the old rule was quietly irritating a lot of people.

Humphrey Yang

Humphrey Yang@humphreytalks

Here's how much you need to make to comfortably afford your rent, and most people are using the wrong number. So the old rule says that rent should be 30% of your gross salary. So $3,000 a month in rent means you need $120,000 per year.

Watch on TikTok (346,410 views, 11,862 likes, 271 comments, 1,048 shares). His replacement rule is 35% of take home pay.

The replacement changes the answer more than it sounds. A $3,000 rent under his math calls for roughly $135,000 of annual income, and at $6,000 a month the gap between the old rule and his rule stretches to nearly $60,000. He caps the whole basket of necessities, rent plus transport plus insurance and recurring costs, at 50 to 55% of income, a ceiling that quietly disqualifies a large share of city centre listings. It is the least romantic advice on this page and possibly the most useful, a single fraction that tells a renter whether the flat is affordable before the viewing is even booked.

Humphrey then turns the same arithmetic on ownership, in a video watched 265,988 times with 14,676 likes.

"Take a 700 thousand dollar loan at a 6.5% interest rate. Over the first 10 years, you'll pay the bank about 531 thousand dollars. But of that, $424,000 is interest and only 106,000 is equity."Humphrey Yang (watch on TikTok)

Four of every five dollars in the first decade buy no ownership at all, before tax, insurance and maintenance, none of which build equity either. His point is not that renting always wins, since a locked in 3% rate plus appreciation rewrites the comparison, but that the renter who ran these numbers and kept investing is doing maths, not wasting money.

Erika Kullberg stages the mortgage debate as a skit with a banker, and the video was watched 158,135 times with 9,922 likes and 943 shares. The banker urges patience and minimums. Her character reaches for ten dollars a day.

Erika Kullberg

Erika Kullberg@erikakullberg

Actually, if I pay an extra $10 a day, I'll end up saving $137,000 in interest alone and the house will be mine in 23 years instead of 30. Who taught you this? Erica taught me. She's a lawyer and has my back when it comes to money.

Watch on TikTok (158,135 views, 9,922 likes, 132 comments). A $450,000 loan at 6% over 30 years.

The arithmetic is the quiet shock of the piece. Ten dollars a day erases seven years and $137,000 in interest from a $450,000 loan at 6%. As a lawyer turned money educator, Erika frames overpayment as self defence against a contract priced to reward the minimum payer. Small daily amounts beat occasional lump sums, because they compound against the interest from day one.

Graham Stephan supplies the market timing, and his housing video drew 146,142 views with 8,220 likes. Where the others argue about personal readiness, he argues the market itself has shifted under their feet.

Graham Stephan

Graham Stephan@grahamstephan

For the first time in almost 20 years, the housing market has started to fall. 47 out of the top 50 cities are getting weaker. Listing prices are now lower than they were in 2024. And over the last few weeks, searches for can't sell a house and help with mortgage just hit an all time record high.

Watch on TikTok (146,142 views, 8,220 likes, 56 comments, 836 shares). Pandemic boom markets unwinding.

His mechanism is supply and demand running in reverse. Builders expanded into the pandemic hotspots just as migration slowed, affordability worsened and insurance costs climbed, so sellers are listing into a market with fewer buyers. As a former agent, he reads the search data as distress. For would be buyers, falling listings plus motivated sellers is the closest thing this market offers to a discount season.

Noah Kettle starts the story a decade before the purchase, in a skit where an 18 year old walks in asking for a house and walks out owning an 815 credit score. The video drew 29,357 views with 1,576 likes, modest beside his viral travel pieces, but it is the most actionable three minutes on this page for anyone under 25.

Noah Kettle

Noah Kettle@businesswithnoah

Hi. I just turned 18, and I'd like to buy a house. 18? Listen, kid, why don't you wait a few years? There's no way you have good enough credit. Oh, really? Take a look at this. An 8 15 credit score.

Watch on TikTok (29,357 views, 1,576 likes, 46 comments). The trick is authorised user piggybacking.

The trick is piggybacking, added as an authorised user to a parent's cards at 15 and building three years of history without ever borrowing. Credit is planted years before it is harvested, and the buyers with the cheapest mortgages started preparing while still in school. Noah's wider catalogue, including a relocation video watched 586,426 times, keeps returning to the same theme, that the cheapest version of adult life goes to whoever reads the small print earliest.

"When I turned 15, my dad called his credit card company and added me as an authorized user to two of his cards. And for the last three years, I've been building credit without ever even having a car."Noah Kettle (watch on TikTok)

Jessica Spangler, known as ecommjess, hunts the discounts most first time buyers never hear about, and her healthcare worker video was watched 84,806 times with 3,830 likes and 1,273 shares, a share count that suggests viewers kept sending it to exactly the friends who qualify.

Jessica Spangler

Jessica Spangler@ecommjess

Oh wow, this house is expensive. Got any discounts for healthcare workers? Actually yeah. There are financial assistance programs to help healthcare workers buy a home. There's down payment assistance, grants up to $8,000, preferred mortgage rates, money back at closing, free home appraisal.

Watch on TikTok (84,806 views, 3,830 likes, 74 comments). Programmes include Homes for Heroes and state grants.

The qualifying list is longer than it looks, running from nursing assistants and lab techs through vets, physicians and pharmacists, and the stack combines in ways buyers miss when they only talk to one lender. Down payment help plus a preferred rate plus cash back at closing can move the deposit math by five figures, which is why her closer matters most, ask local banks about their own schemes, because the quiet local grant is often the one with money still in it.

Dahlia closes the loop from the other side of completion day, a new homeowner furnishing empty rooms, and her video may be the smallest here at 1,721 views, but it is the only one filmed with the keys already in hand.

Dahlia

Dahlia@getontopwithdahlia

Here are three money habits the banks absolutely hate me for, but save me thousands of pounds a year. Number one is bank switch offers. For those of you that were just born today, these are when the bank pays you money to switch your current account over to them.

Watch on TikTok (1,721 views, 82 likes). Habit two funded her new house furnishing.

Switch bonuses near £200 a time, repeated every few months, compound to around £1,000 a year. The second habit paid for the housewarming directly. Facing an empty new house, she put the furnishing on her card, then shifted the balance onto an interest free transfer deal, a free loan for a year with a plan to roll it again if the balance outlives the offer. The third habit rotates the emergency fund yearly toward whichever bank pays the best introductory rate.

"As I just bought my new house and I had to furnish the entire thing in one go because I had no furniture, I did have to put a bit on my credit card and I use the card that I already have and switch the entire balance over to a brand new not percent card. Meaning that I'm paying no interest on that money."Dahlia (watch on TikTok)

Where they agree, and where they split

On the core claim there is no daylight. Every creator here prices housing with actual numbers instead of slogans, and every one of them distrusts a rule of thumb until the arithmetic confirms it. Humphrey replaces 30% of gross with 35% of take home. Erika prices the $10 against $137,000. Graham counts weakening cities. Noah prices the score. Jess prices the grants. Dahlia prices the switch bonus. The unanimous vote is that the front door has a number on it, and the number is knowable before you commit.

"I'm not telling you that renting always wins, but sometimes throwing money away on rent is actually cheaper than throwing it away on interest."Humphrey Yang (watch on TikTok)
"No, no need. Just take your time. Pay the minimum amount due each month."Erika Kullberg (watch on TikTok)

The split opens on what to do once the numbers are known. Humphrey's camp says renting on purpose is legitimate maths, especially when early mortgage years buy almost no equity. The buyers camp, Jess and Dahlia and Noah at its centre, says ownership rewards the prepared, with grants, transfer deals and teenage credit history doing the quiet work. Erika's skit stages the second split inside the mortgage itself, the banker's minimums against her ten dollars a day, and nobody on this page defends paying $137,000 of avoidable interest. Graham stands slightly apart, arguing that timing the market cycle matters as much as personal readiness.

The practical close is a sequence, not a slogan. Price the rent against take home pay at 35% and the full necessities basket at 55%, and rent deliberately if ownership maths fails. Start the credit file years before the application, and ask every assistance scheme, national and local, what it will contribute. If buying, overpay early and automatically, even in lunch money amounts, because early dollars kill the most interest. Then keep switching, transferring and rotating everything the bank hoped would stay put, since Dahlia's keys prove the boring habits compound into front doors.
