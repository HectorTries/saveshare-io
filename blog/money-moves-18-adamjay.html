What a 24-year-old accountant wishes he'd started at 18

← Blog Getting started

Adam Jay (Adam Jay), a 24-year-old chartered accountant, looks back at 18 with the crisp regret of a man who has seen the spreadsheets. His most watched reflection, posted to just over 3,000 viewers, compresses the whole philosophy into a single minute of camera talk:

adm.jay

adm.jay7.0K followers on TikTok

"Five financial goals to reach before 30, coming from a 24 year old accountant who wish he started these things at 18."

Watch on TikTok

The five goals arrive with accountant precision. First, a credit score above 861, the threshold the agencies call excellent. Second, zero interest bearing credit card debt, not zero cards, but zero interest paid. Third, at least £10,000 inside a Stocks and Shares ISA. Fourth, an emergency fund covering three to six months of living expenses. Fifth, and the one he repeats most urgently, full use of the workplace pension employer match from the very first payslip. In his words:

adm.jay7.0K followers on TikTok

"Number one, your credit score is higher than 861. No.3, you have invested at least £10,000 into your stocks and shares, ISA. No. 4, you have built an emergency fund of at least three to six months of living expenses. And No. 5, you are matching out your current workplace pension, employer match."

Watch on TikTok

What gives the list its sting is the property maths sitting behind it. In a separate video, watched by more than 1,200 people, Adam walks through the deposit arithmetic facing every 18-year-old who dreams of owning a home. The average UK house price sits around £270,000, a 10 percent deposit means finding £27,000, and the mortgage left over demands an income most teenagers cannot picture. As he puts it:

adm.jay7.0K followers on TikTok

"So the average house price in the UK is around £270,000. And for this example, we are going to assume that you need a 10% deposit, which would be £27,000. You would need to earn £54,000 a year to afford the average house in the UK."

Watch on TikTok

The £54,000 figure, derived from banks lending around four and a half times salary, lands like a verdict. Knowing the target at 18, Adam argues, lets a teenager aim a career and a Lifetime ISA at it deliberately, instead of discovering the gap at 28. On the Lifetime ISA he is evangelical, and precise:

adm.jay7.0K followers on TikTok

"The first reason to open a lifetime ISA is that the government pays you a 25% bonus on anything you save. So if you save the maximum 4,000 pounds a year, the government would give you a free 1,000 pound bonus."

Watch on TikTok

Enter DiDi Finance (DiDi), who supplies what Adam implies: the compounding tables that turn early starts into destiny. Her millionaire maths video, her breakout at more than 13,000 views, assumes a 7 percent average yearly return and then asks what monthly sum retires you a millionaire depending on when you begin. The answer is the whole argument for starting at 18:

didifinance

didifinanceon TikTok

"At the age of 50, you would need to invest £3,100 every month. At the age of 40, you would need to invest £1,250. At the age of 30, you would need to invest £535. At the age of 25, you would need to invest £350. And at the age of 18, you would need to invest just £190 every month."

Watch on TikTok

She hammers the moral herself, in the same breathless cadence that makes her videos so shareable:

didifinanceon TikTok

"For every 10 years you put off investing, you have to invest almost double the amount you did 10 years ago to make a million. And that is the power of compound growth. The earlier you start, the more your money can work for you."

Watch on TikTok

DiDi runs the same logic at smaller stakes for the sceptic who hears millionaire and stops listening. Invest £100 a month for 30 years at 7 percent, she shows, and £36,000 of contributions becomes £117,000. Raise it to £500 and £180,000 becomes £585,000. Her framing is deliberate:

didifinanceon TikTok

"What happens if you invest just £100 every month for 30 years? We are gonna assume an annual average rate of return of 7%. You would have invested £36,000, but you would have made £117,000."

Watch on TikTok

Where Adam prescribes the global index fund and leaves it there, DiDi delights in the menu. Told that every beginner gets pointed at the American S and P 500, she filmed a riposte viewed nearly 6,000 times, five alternative exchange traded funds for anyone who wants something else. Her explainer of the instrument itself is among the clearest on this side of TikTok:

didifinanceon TikTok

"Here are five etfs that you should invest in that are not the SMP 500. An ETF is an exchange traded fund. It is just a basket of thousands of companies into one single stock that you can buy."

And here the two begin to diverge in temperament, which is where Janice (Janice) walks in with the receipts. A 22 year old graduate, watched by 34,900 people in her flagship video, she left university with £10,000 already in her investment portfolio. Not through a graduate salary or a parental gift, but through a part time job and a standing order. Her retelling is disarmingly plain:

On the question of where to hold all this, Janice and Adam sing in unison, and Janice gives the ISA its most memorable slogan. Britain taxes nearly everything, she notes, except this one gleaming exception:

"So there is actually a reason why people call ISAs the U K's way to getting rich. This is because in a country where literally everything is taxed, this is the one thing that is not."

So far, so harmonious: open the ISA at 18, automate something, let decades work. The fourth voice complicates the picture, usefully. Sophie Blank (Sophie Blank), an AI strategist who has spent eight years in big tech, agrees with the destination but disputes the vehicle. Systems, not willpower, carry her finances, and her system has a name: sinking funds, separate pots preloaded monthly for holidays, gifts, bills and surprises, so predictable expenses never touch a credit card. Her claim, made to 2,000 viewers, is absolute:

itssophieblank

itssophieblankon TikTok

"My sinking funds are the one reason I pay off my credit card every single month and I never go into debt while building my credit score and paying for things like summer holidays, Christmas presents and any other surprises."

Watch on TikTok

itssophieblankon TikTok

"Christmas does not sneak up on you in November, and September's holiday is already funded in June. I have separate pots for holidays, gifts, bills, wellness shopping. Whatever happens, I am covered."

Watch on TikTok

This is the genuine disagreement at the heart of the money advice chorus, and it is worth naming. Adam preaches the emergency fund as a lump sum target, three to six months of expenses, a fortress to build. Sophie preaches many small earmarked pots, funded monthly, a plumbing system to install. Adam optimises for the catastrophe, Sophie for the calendar. An 18-year-old following Adam saves toward one large number and may watch Christmas spending wreck the plan. An 18-year-old following Sophie funds the year in advance and may never build the large buffer. The synthesis, which neither quite states, is to run both: automate the ISA for decades, automate the pots for December.

A second fault line runs through lifestyle. Adam, the accountant, itemises restraint: no interest paid, ever. DiDi, by contrast, has planted her flag as the anti frugality creator, documenting wealth building while openly enjoying her twenties. Her manifesto video states the rebellion plainly:

didifinanceon TikTok

"I am tired of the finance space telling everyone to be frugal and not spend their money, because not everyone wants to do that. So we are here to build intentional wealth while still enjoying our life."

Watch on TikTok

Sophie lands on the same side of this divide from a different direction. Her proudest boast is not a balance but a feeling, that she never feels guilty spending, because the pots already hold the money. And her worldview has a harder edge still, delivered in her shortest video:

itssophieblankon TikTok

"This world rewards exactly one thing, and that is audacity."

Watch on TikTok

Read together, the four creators form a complete machine with interlocking parts. Adam supplies the targets and the tax wrappers, the £10,000 ISA goal, the pension match, the Lifetime ISA bonus, the forbidding £54,000 property arithmetic that makes the rest urgent. DiDi supplies the mathematical proof that time beats size, £190 at 18 against £3,100 at 50, and the fund menu for the curious. Janice supplies the on ramp, living evidence that £2 grows into five figures when a student automates it and leaves it alone. Sophie supplies the plumbing that keeps the machine from seizing, pots that convert lumpy annual costs into smooth monthly flows so the credit card stays at zero without a single act of willpower.

Run the rough numbers that unite them and the case closes itself. Take £25 a month compounding at 7 percent in real terms from 18 to 68. The contributions total £15,000 across a lifetime. The pot lands near £120,000 in present money. Double the standing order to £50 and the destination approaches a quarter of a million. The direction is the message: the lost years compound longest, so the start date matters more than the amount.

The practical synthesis for an 18-year-old, distilled from all four voices, runs something like this. Open a Stocks and Shares ISA in the week you turn 18 and automate any amount into a global index fund, even £10, because Janice proves the floor is pennies and DiDi proves the ceiling is millions. Route the Lifetime ISA decision through Adam's 25 percent bonus logic if a first home is the goal. Build Sophie's sinking funds alongside the emergency buffer, one pot per predictable expense, plus one pot for the unpredictable, so the credit card that builds the 861 score never carries a balance. Join the workplace pension from the first payslip and never opt out, because the employer match is the only free money Adam refuses to leave on the table. And check the high yield corners DiDi patrols, like the account paying 6.5 percent on £300 a month that turns a year of saving into £3,700 plus £125 of interest, for the cash that must stay liquid.

Open the ISA at 18, automate anything, pot the predictable, match the pension, and let decades do the rest. Four creators, four temperaments, one verdict: the amount barely matters, the start date is everything.
