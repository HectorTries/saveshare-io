From £23K Debt to £400K Net Worth: The Debt to Investing Pipeline

Kill the expensive debt, build the system, then let contributions do the heavy lifting

By SaveShare.io · 19 October 2026

Kill the expensive debt, build the system, then let contributions do the work.

Back to Blog Debt Investing UK

Every payday, before anything enjoyable happens, Hannah Bevington checks she was paid correctly, clears any high interest debt in full, moves money into spending pots, and invests whatever is left. Some months that is £50. Other months it is two or three thousand. The amount changes. The order never does. Five years of running this sequence made investing, in her words, the best thing she ever did, and a video explaining a parallel payday habit drew almost 40,000 views.

Hannah Bevington

Hannah Bevington@hannahbevington

The really unfortunate thing about building wealth is it does not just happen. You really have to curate it and put certain systems in place to ensure you get there. The second thing I do is I pay off any high interest debts. There is nothing that pains me more than paying interest. The fourth thing that I do, I invest. I always just invest whatever I can. Sometimes it looks like £50 a month, other times it looks like two, three, four thousand.

Watch on TikTok (the payday sequence, debt first then invest).

The tension this page addresses is the one that splits beginners into warring camps. Clear every debt before investing a penny, or invest while paying down. Hannah's answer is a sequence rather than a side, expensive debt first, then systems, then investing, every single payday in that order. The creators below each own one stage of that pipeline, and one of them has ridden it all the way to a £400,000 net worth at 33.

Hannah's defence against ever rejoining the debt camp is a set of spending pots inside her Starling account, separate pots for Christmas, holidays and other occasions, funded monthly so predictable expenses never land on a credit card. The method gives her autonomy over where the money goes instead of spending with fingers crossed. Her framing inverts the usual moralising, because the pots mean she never feels guilty about spending money she has already set aside.

"The third thing I do is I put my money into spending pots. So one of the banks which I bank with is Starling and I basically have different pots for different occasions, so like Christmas, holidays. Just helps me have a bit more autonomy over where my money is actually going, rather than just spending it, cross my fingers and hoping for the best."Hannah Bevington (watch on TikTok)

His payoff story drew 15,703 views, and the follow up on staying debt free drew 32,474 with 1,513 likes, proof that the audience cares as much about the aftermath as the escape.

Jake's diagnosis of his own debt is unusually honest about aimlessness. He did not buy a house, was not saving for a car, had no goals pulling money toward the future, so spending absorbed everything. His fix is behavioural rather than mathematical, a waiting rule for every non essential purchase. Sit on the desire for a few weeks and watch most of it expire. Readers who want his full method will find it alongside other young savers in the young grinders guide, where his story sits next to Mia, Trell and Sean.

"If I saw something I liked it, I bought it, did not care about how I was going to pay for it. I think almost everything I have bought over the past six years I do not have anymore and that is the truth. So now I am trying to be more conscious about things I buy. I sort of sit on it for a little bit and if after a few weeks, even a few months, if I still want it, maybe then I can look into purchasing it."Jake Shuttleworth (watch on TikTok)

Once the expensive debt is gone, Neil Invests takes over the argument. His core claim is disarmingly simple and easy to get wrong. For years, what you put in matters far more than what the market does with it. Ten percent on £1,000 is a hundred pounds. The same ten percent on £100,000 is life changing. The gap between those two positions is built from contributions, which means it is built from income, patience and habit rather than cleverness.

Neil Invests

Neil Invests@neilinvests

The biggest thing that is gonna impact your portfolio performance for a number of years is your contributions into it, not the returns that you gonna make. Because if you make 10 percent on £1,000, which is great, that is a hundred quid. You gotta keep going. You gotta get to a point where you got a bit more than that.

Watch on TikTok (11,926 views, contributions beat returns).

His own method for raising contributions was deliberately boring. Move companies, niche down, increase income, funnel the surplus into a global tracker rather than individual stock bets. He argues that matching the whole market beats most investors, because most investors underperform the market by trying to outsmart it. His £350,000 portfolio video, watched 26,115 times, extends the same logic into retirement maths, asking whether the pot could sustain withdrawals and answering with unusual candour.

"I just wanna match the all world. If I do that, I actually will beat most investors, because most investors underperform the all world by trying to beat it. I identified early on that the best way for me to do it was by increasing my income and putting as much in as I could."Neil Invests (watch on TikTok)

Her explanation is structural rather than motivational. A high salary kept savings rates extreme. Low housing costs, via the deliberate choice to live with parents, kept the surplus intact. A pension she tracks across four employers compounded underneath everything. Her day in the life video, watched 21,186 times, and her three step investing explainer with 16,049 views and 97 comments, show the same temperament applied to ordinary weeks. No single decision made her wealthy. The system did.

"I am 33, earn £165,000 a year and I live at home with my parents because I choose to. And I wanna talk about this in the context of high agency. High agency is the ability and mindset to actively shape your own life. And there are three core traits of this I absolutely have, which is why I live a life on my own terms. The first is a bias to action."Tatiana (watch on TikTok)

Where they agree, and where they split

On sequencing there is no real disagreement. Every voice here puts expensive consumer debt before ambitious investing. Hannah clears cards every payday before investing a penny of the remainder. Jake cleared £23,000 before thinking about portfolios. Neil's contributions sermon assumes money that is not being eaten by interest. Tatiana's sprint was possible because no expensive debt skimmed the surplus. The unanimous vote is that interest paid to lenders is the first leak to plug.

The split opens on how much to enjoy along the way. Hannah treats herself to a £100 gift monthly, buys a daily coffee and gets her nails done, arguing in a video watched 11,713 times that she works hard and will enjoy her income without apology. Jake's method leans stricter, weeks of waiting before any non essential purchase. Tatiana's low housing costs look like sacrifice but she frames them as agency, a chosen trade rather than a deprivation. The awkward truth is that all three built wealth while spending on something, which suggests the sustainable plan beats the austere one, provided the sequence holds.

The second split is income versus frugality as the engine. Neil and Tatiana emphasise raising earnings, moving companies, niching down, commanding a high salary, because contributions scale with income. Jake and Hannah emphasise controlling outflows, waiting rules and sinking funds, because leaks sink every boat regardless of salary. Both engines work and the fastest results combine them, which is exactly what Tatiana did, high income captured by low costs. For readers choosing one lever first, the weight of voices says plug the leaks this month and chase the raise this year.

The practical close follows Hannah's order. This payday, clear the expensive balances first and price the guilt out of future spending with one sinking fund, yearly cost divided by twelve. Next, add Jake's waiting rule to non essentials and automate a small investment in the style of Neil, even a modest one, because contributions compound before returns do. Hold the sequence for years and the endpoint looks like Tatiana, survival money converted into a system, the system converted into six figures. For readers who also want the rest and balance side of wealth, Rebecca's invest and rest philosophy shows what the pipeline compounds into when the goal becomes early retirement rather than the next milestone. Different stages, different salaries, identical pipeline, kill the debt, build the system, feed it monthly.
