Red on the Screen: Five Creators on Surviving the Dip Without Selling

Fellas on the 108,000-view downturn guide, Steve on buying more shares at lower prices, Charlie down £10,000 in a month, Trell on the midterm dip and Iain on three rules

Fellas Finance posted his video on the 24th of October, days into a slide that had turned plenty of portfolios an unfamiliar colour. The market had been down for a month, and for anyone who started investing recently this was probably the first time they had watched their own money shrink. His opening line names the feeling directly, that sinking question of what exactly you have signed up for. The video has since been watched 108,300 times with 4,184 likes, which suggests the feeling was widely shared. He filmed it because the red on the screen was doing something to new investors that no explainer about compound growth had prepared them for, and he wanted to get to them before they pressed sell.

The tension underneath every voice on this page is the oldest one in investing, and the hardest to hold steady when the numbers move. The rational case says a falling market is a sale, cheaper shares for the monthly buyer, time smoothing every dip if you wait long enough. The emotional case says the red means you were wrong, that the money was safer where it was, that selling now at least stops the pain. Fellas, Steve, Charlie, Trell and Iain all stood in front of the same red screen in October and November and filmed the same decision from five different angles. None of them sold. What they disagree about is what to do instead, and how loudly to say it.

Fellas Finance leads because his video is the calmest room in a loud week, and the most watched clip on this page by a distance. Filming from the UK to an audience of new investors, he starts with permission to feel bad, then moves to the arithmetic that makes feeling bad unnecessary. His framing is deliberately boring, and that is the point.

Fellas Finance

Fellas Finance@fellasfinance

Stock market crashes, corrections and downturns are completely normal. Stock market goes up and down every single day, and it expects to have a correction roughly every two years and a crash every seven years. This is not easy to get your head around, but it is completely normal. When people say investing is simple and easy, it is. Until things like this happen. This is something you are just going to have to embrace.

Watch on TikTok (108,300 views, 4,184 likes). Filmed days into the October slide, his guide for first time investors seeing red.

His second move is to widen the frame until the red shrinks. Yes, the market is down over the week and the month, he says, but over twelve months it is up 13 percent, a very good year. A dip means the next monthly contribution buys more of the shares you were buying anyway. He calls downturns something to embrace, and draws the line between people who stop investing and people who become successful with it.

Steve Chen filmed the same week from the US side, and his video, watched 58,400 times with 1,519 likes, reads like the American cousin of the Fellas script. Where Fellas is soothing, Steve is blunt about the noise, and his villains are specific.

Steve Chen

Steve Chen@calltoleap

A lot of people are saying there is going to be a recession or a bubble burst. And I always see experts predicting the next market crash on top of finance gurus scaring you into selling everything. But long term investors like myself know that this is all clickbait, that stock market drops are normal and this is the best time to become rich. In your adult life, you will most likely see five to 10 large stock market drops, but really these are the greatest opportunities to build wealth.

Watch on TikTok (58,400 views, 1,519 likes). His October message on crash predictions, consistency and index funds.

Steve names the mechanism Fellas only implies. During drops, he says, rich people invest more while everyone else panics, and when the market recovers the patient portfolios skyrocket while the panickers miss the move. His prescription is deliberately plain, low cost broad index funds, consistent contributions, negative news switched off. The line that carries the video is the one about clickbait, because it tells a scared beginner that the fear itself is the product being sold.

Charlie is the only creator on this page showing his own red numbers instead of describing red numbers in general. Filming his November portfolio update as a 28 year old with more than £100,000 invested, he opens with the damage report and refuses to flinch. The clip is small, just 1,253 views and 44 likes so far, which makes it the most honest video here, posted for the record rather than the algorithm.

Charlie

Charlie@wealthmadeaccessible

The stock market is currently crashing, experiencing a correction, whatever you want to call it. Which means only one thing we need to do a portfolio update for November. So let us get started with my invest account. Since I did my last update last month, we are down £7,118 which is down 9.1 percent. Here is the thing, I am not actually too worried about the current correction that we are experiencing.

Watch on TikTok (1,253 views, 44 likes). His November update, down £7,118 in one account and £5,936 in his ISA.

The reason he is not worried is the zoom out, done live on camera. The invest account still shows a 44.6 percent return, still up £4,700. The ISA sits at £75,134, down £5,936 in the month, yet up 164.4 percent since he started, more than £13,000 in profit. Total portfolio, £147,553, down more than £10,000 since October and still up nearly £20,000 overall. Then the old line, delivered without ceremony. When other people are scared, be greedy.

Trell enters at full volume, and his video, watched 67,000 times with 7,681 likes, is the most aggressive buy signal on the page. Filming in early November after the midterm elections knocked AI and data stocks down hard, he frames the dip as a scheduled sale on names his audience already wanted at full price.

Trell

Trell@trellthetrainer

What you do when the market is down is far more important than what you do when the market is up. I have been telling you all this for months. And everybody wanted these same stocks when it was up at all time highs. And now guess what. Everybody is panic selling. When people panic sell, that is the opportune time for you to go and buy these stocks. Because you get them at a discount.

Watch on TikTok (67,000 views, 7,681 likes). His November dip buying guide, VTI and QQQM plus the sale watchlist.

His instructions are concrete where the others stay philosophical. Open a brokerage account today, Fidelity or Schwab or Vanguard, fifteen minutes. Move at least $2,000 across, then buy the boring base first, VTI for thousands of US companies in one holding, QQQM for big tech on sale, with Bloom Energy, AMD and Amazon on the watchlist. Underneath the salesmanship sits the same doctrine, panic selling transfers wealth from the frightened to the patient. Trell just says it louder, and attaches tickers.

Iain Geddes closes the circle with the most structured version of the argument, three tips filmed against his own sea of red. His video shows the assets page, all red, down £2,300 in a single day, and then he talks the viewer through it like a drill.

Iain Geddes

Iain Geddes@iainmoneyinsights

Tip number one, do not panic sell. Do not panic sell your stocks, your investments. Remember why you invested in them in the first place. Think of that long term picture. When stocks drop like this, you are tempted to sell to stop the pain. But just remember that is usually the worst time to sell. Instead, ask yourself, why did I buy this. Has the reason for buying this investment changed.

Watch on TikTok. His three tip crash survival guide, filmed with his own red portfolio on screen.

His second tip is the proverb, time in the market beats timing the market, demonstrated with his own figures. Down £2,300 on the day, yet up £5,234 on the month. His third tip turns the red into a task, rebalance, review goals, add only if it fits the plan. Where Trell shouts buy, Iain says review. One is an order, the other is a question, and beginners need the question first.

Where they agree, and where they split

On the core claim there is no daylight anywhere on this page. All five treat a falling market as normal weather, not a verdict, and all five give the same order, do not sell into it. Fellas says corrections arrive every two years and crashes every seven, Steve says five to ten large drops per adult lifetime, Iain says ask whether the reason you bought has changed, and since it has not, hold. Charlie proves it with a portfolio still up nearly £20,000 after a £10,000 month. Trell prices it as a discount on stocks the audience wanted at the top. The unanimous vote is that the red screen is a test of behaviour, not information, and selling turns a paper move into a permanent one.

The split opens on what to do with your hands while you hold. Trell says move money now, open the account, buy VTI and QQQM while the sale lasts. Steve says keep the monthly contributions going in broad index funds and switch off the news. Fellas lands between them, keep placing the monthly investment and let lower prices do the work. Iain is the most cautious, rebalance and review first. Charlie is the living proof that doing nothing works, his gains survived on contributions and patience. Where Trell sees a trade, Iain sees a test, Fellas sees tuition, Steve sees clickbait, and Charlie sees content.

The deepest disagreement is about tone. Trell shouts because his viewers need activation energy. Fellas soothes because his are first timers seeing red for the first time. Steve warns because his are drowning in predictions. Iain teaches because his want a checklist. Charlie just shows the numbers, because a man down £10,000 in a month and still calm teaches faster than any script. Five volumes, one sentence, stay invested.

Nobody here claims the dip feels good. Fellas admits it is not easy to get your head around. Steve admits seeing the account go down does not feel good. Charlie films the £7,118 hole before he films the reassurance. Iain puts his own red assets on screen. Trell is the only one who sounds like he is enjoying himself, and even he took the hit first. The practical close is unglamorous, which is the point. Decide why you bought before the next dip, automate the monthly contribution so courage is not required, zoom out to twelve months before judging one, and treat spare cash in a downturn as a discount on the shares you already wanted. The market will fall again, roughly every two years by Fellas count. The five voices here will be filming again too, red on the screen, hands off the sell button.
