Don't Fear Market Dips: Lupes on ETFs and Buying the Fall

Why falling markets are a gift to monthly investors

Why falling markets are a gift to monthly investors

← Blog Investing

The panic always arrives on the same schedule. Markets slide, phone screens turn red, and a generation of first time investors stares at numbers moving the wrong way and wonders whether to sell everything before it gets worse. In late July, as headline chip stocks wobbled and her inbox filled with frightened messages asking if this was the moment to get out, Lupes (Lupes) picked up her phone and said the opposite of what everyone felt. The dip was not the danger, she argued. Misunderstanding it was.

Her timing was pointed. Micron, one of the great winners of the AI rally, had fallen around 25 percent in a month even after smashing earnings. Viewers who had cheered the climb were suddenly asking whether the stock would ever recover. Lupes saw something familiar in the fear: people reacting to the price without understanding the business underneath. The red days feel like evidence that something is broken, and for the monthly investor they are usually evidence of the opposite, a sale on the same assets at lower prices.

This is the founding tension of every dip conversation on money TikTok. Your eyes tell you that falling prices mean losing. The arithmetic of regular investing tells you that falling prices mean your fixed monthly payment buys more shares, and those cheap shares do the heaviest compounding over the decades that follow. One of those stories is emotional and immediate. The other is mathematical and slow. Four British creators, each with a different audience and a different style, have been circling the same conclusion from four directions, and their agreement is worth more than any single pep talk.

That declaration, delivered to nearly 97,000 viewers, sounds like hype until you hear the reasoning behind it. Lupes is not a purist. She holds individual stocks alongside her funds, seven investments in her Stocks and Shares ISA, and she is candid that a single stock can sprint 30 percent in a day while an ETF plods. Her point is narrower and more practical: for beginners, and for the core of anyone's portfolio, one low cost global ETF does nearly all the work. It holds thousands of companies in a single purchase, it needs no stock picking skill, and it keeps compounding while you get on with your life. Everything else is decoration around that foundation.

Her most quoted video on the subject makes the dip argument explicit, and it is worth reading closely because it names the exact mistake panicking investors make. When everything falls at once, she says, the fall cannot be about the individual companies you own. It is sentiment, not analysis, and sentiment reverses.

Note the quiet detail buried in that video. While viewers fretted over single stocks down a quarter, her global tracker was down about 1 percent on the month. Diversification does not prevent falls, but it shrinks them to a scale where patience is plausible. That is the whole ballgame: construct a portfolio whose worst months you can tolerate, then keep buying through them, and the dips turn from threat into discount.

If Lupes supplies the philosophy, DiDi Finance (DiDi) supplies the shopping list. Her most useful video starts from an observation every beginner has had: everyone tells you to buy the S and P 500, but what if you do not want to? What follows is a brisk tour of five alternatives, from the FTSE 100 at home to tech heavy Nasdaq trackers, emerging markets, developed world funds and Japan. The specific tickers matter less than the mental model underneath, which she states with unusual clarity for a 115 second video.

Read that definition twice, because it contains the answer to the dip question. If your holding is a basket of thousands of companies, a falling market does not mean your companies are broken. It means the basket got cheaper. The strategy is diversification plus persistence. The ticker is a detail.

Then comes the voice this story has been missing: the absolute beginner with twenty pounds and no idea what any of it means. Janice (Janice), a 22 year old graduate, filmed the video she wished someone had filmed for her: a step by step account of opening an account and investing a first £20. It has been watched more than 167,000 times, which tells you how large the audience for permission to start small really is. Her opening line does half the work of the whole genre.

"So you've got a spare £20 lying around and you wanna know how to invest it, but you don't know how. Let me show you what I would do as a 22 year old grad with over £10,000 invested into the stock market."

Watch on TikTok

Janice's mechanics deserve attention because they are the dip strategy in miniature. She tells beginners to open a Stocks and Shares ISA first, before buying anything, so that every future gain sits inside a tax free wrapper. The order matters: account, then funding, then fund choice, never the reverse. Her reasoning is blunt and correct.

"A stocks and shares ISA is a really good way to go. This is because you get £20,000 which is tax free. And this means that any gains that you get on the stocks that you have, you won't have to pay tax on."Janice via TikTok

And then the detail that separates investors from spectators: she automated everything. In a companion video watched nearly 35,000 times, she explains that she left university with a five figure portfolio built from a £100 monthly standing order plus whatever survived the month, invested only in ETFs and index funds while she learned. Small, boring, automatic. The automation is doing quiet double duty here. It builds the habit, and it guarantees that when dips arrive you are buying without needing courage, because the decision was made months ago.

"I would automate an amount that I would put into my trading 212 account every month. I chose £100 cause I had a part time job at the time. But you could literally do it with any amount."Janice via TikTok

The fourth voice belongs to Ollie Invests (Ollie Invests), and he brings the spreadsheet. Where Lupes preaches and Janice guides, Ollie calculates. His portfolio walkthrough lays out the textbook allocation in plain numbers: the bulk in a global tracker, a slice in a second growth fund, and small hedges in physical gold and silver against inflation. Then he runs the compounding math that turns pocket money into a future: £50 a week, invested for decades, growing into a sum worth hundreds of thousands. The projection assumes generous returns and he says so himself, but the direction is the point. Small, repeated, diversified.

His exact allocation shows what diversification looks like once you move past your first fund.

"So as you can see, 70% of my money goes straight into a footsie world with the distributing fund. Another 20% goes into J G Z I, which is also a global growth fund. And then I put 5% into physical gold and 5% into physical silver as my hedge against inflation."Ollie Invests via TikTok

And here is the honest coda that keeps him credible: the portfolio is not large yet, and he does not pretend otherwise. His horizon is ten, twenty, thirty years. The dip does not threaten that plan. The plan requires the dips.

"But I just put my money into this every month and try to increase it. And also at the minute you can see it's not a massive portfolio size, but I'm hoping to grow this over the long term and intend to invest into this for 10, 20, 30 years."Ollie Invests via TikTok

Put the four voices side by side and the contrasts are as instructive as the chorus. On single stocks, Lupes and Janice visibly disagree, or rather, they speak from different chapters of the same book. Lupes trades individual names with enthusiasm and counts a five month 11 percent gain on one position as proof of the upside. University age Janice did the opposite: she avoided the high flyers entirely, sticking only to ETFs and index funds, and touched single stocks only after her foundation was built. There is no contradiction here, only sequencing. Janice describes chapter one. Lupes is living in chapter three. The beginner who copies chapter three first has skipped the part where survival is learned.

On fund choice, DiDi and Lupes complement each other. Lupes says one global ETF is enough to start. DiDi offers five doors instead of one, mapping regions and themes so viewers can choose with intent. Both reduce to the same rule: pick a diversified basket and hold it.

On history, the record is generous to all of them. The 2008 financial crisis, the 2020 Covid crash, the 2022 inflation slump: each looked terminal in the moment and each recovered to new highs within a few years. Investors who kept buying through 2008 tripled their money within a decade. A century of diversified global equities says the same thing every generation forgets and relearns: broad markets fall often and recover always, given enough time, and the buyers during the falls end up with the best lifetime returns of all. The four creators are not citing this literature. They are living its retail version, month by month, direct debit by direct debit.

"I say all that to say do not fear the dip, especially if you're a long term investor. Stay lively."Lupes, to viewers panicking over a 25 percent single stock fall while her global tracker sat about 1 percent down on the month

The synthesis is almost offensively simple, which is why it keeps needing to be said. Open the ISA. Pick one diversified fund. Automate a monthly payment you can sustain through bad headlines. Then close the app, as Janice puts it, and go do anything else.

So the takeaway for the next red morning is a short one. Check whether your direct debit is still running, whether your money is landing in a diversified fund inside the ISA, and whether you know what you own well enough to hold it. If the answer to all three is yes, the dip is selling you the future at a discount. Stay lively, keep buying, and let the recovery do the talking.
