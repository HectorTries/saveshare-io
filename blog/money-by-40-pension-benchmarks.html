Money by 40: Four Creators on Pension Benchmarks That Tell the Truth

Daniel sets five numbers for 40, Erin coasts at 27, Steve prices freedom at 25 times spending, and Humphrey rebuilds from zero.

Five numbers to hit by 40, one coast milestone at 27, and the maths that connects them across the Atlantic

Daniel films the kind of video that ruins a quiet evening. He stands in front of the camera and reads out the typical pension pot for a Briton in their late forties, and the number is so small that the comments fill with people checking their own balance in a panic. In a September video watched 80,420 times with 633 likes, 235 comments and 100 shares, he walks through salary, cash savings, investments, pensions and debt for people in their forties, and the pension figure lands hardest of all. Among 45 to 54 year olds with a defined contribution pension, the typical combined pot sits between £30,000 and £50,000. That is not a typo and it is not a worst case. It is the middle.

The tension underneath this page is the gap between what the average person has and what the maths says they need. Retirement advice usually arrives as a single magic number, save this much, hit that multiple, retire happy. Real life arrives as childcare bills, rent rises and job changes, and the average pot reflects exactly that friction. Four creators take the same question from opposite ends. Daniel reports the averages and sets targets. Erin, at 27, describes the milestone that freed her from chasing the highest salary. Steve, a former teacher turned millionaire, prices freedom as a multiple of spending. Humphrey rebuilds the whole system from zero with four rules. They agree on the destination and argue, gently, about the route.

Daniel leads because his two benchmark videos are the most shared pension explainers on this page. His September averages video works as a mirror, while his October targets video works as a map, and the pair belong together because the distance between them is the whole story.

Number one is salary. The typical full time salary for someone aged 40 to 49 is around £44,244 and more than that and you are above the U K median for your age. Number two is cash savings. Among 45 to 54 year olds who have savings, the typical amount is only between four and five thousand pounds.

The rest of that video keeps the blows coming. Only around 35 percent of people aged 45 to 54 hold investments at all, and among those who do the typical portfolio runs to £7,000 or £8,000 excluding pensions and property. Around 48 percent carry unsecured debt with a typical balance of £3,800. Read straight, it is bleak. Read properly, it is oddly comforting, because anyone with an emergency fund and a pension already sits above the middle, and Daniel says so outright. You may be doing better than you thought.

"No.4 is pensions. Among 45 to 54 year olds with a defined contribution pension, the typical combined pot is between 30 and 50,000 pounds."

A month later Daniel returned with the companion piece, five numbers to aim for by 40, and it drew 50,400 plays. The pension target is the centrepiece. Aim for twice your annual salary in the pot, so £100,000 on a £50,000 income, with £10,000 of accessible cash savings and £20,000 of investments outside the pension alongside it. Expensive debt goes to zero, excluding the mortgage and student loan. He repeats the caveat twice, that these are benchmarks and not a verdict on a life, and the comments suggest viewers needed to hear it.

If Daniel is the surveyor, Erin Confortini is the proof that starting early rewrites the whole equation. At 27, with eight income streams, she filmed the financial milestones that changed her life, and the video drew 9,271 plays with 550 likes. The second milestone was clearing around $50,000 of student loans and car debt in 18 months using side hustle income. The fourth was the one that matters for this page. She hit the point where her investments could grow to retirement without another dollar added.

Erin Confortini

Erin Confortini@itserinconfortini

The second is when I fully paid off my debt. I had around $50,000 of debt between student loans and a car payment, and I was able to pay that off within 18 months of making money outside of my corporate salary.

Watch on TikTok (9,271 views, 550 likes, 7 comments, 9 shares). Milestones that changed her life at 27.

Her first milestone funded the rest. When her side hustle crossed $1,000 a month she refused to let it inflate her lifestyle, routing it straight into investing and debt payoff instead. Sequence, not salary, is the argument.

"And fourth is when I hit coast. This milestone is when your investments are at a place where if you never invested another dollar, by the time you get to retirement age, the money that is in your investment portfolio will have grown to a point where you have enough in there to be able to retire."

Steve Chen tells the story from the other side of the mountain. A millionaire former teacher who now teaches personal finance for free, he opens with the night his marriage nearly broke over money. Earning $270,000 a year with a lake house and twin infants, he realised the household had one month of real runway. The video drew 7,128 plays with 244 likes, and its power comes from the confession. High income with no reserves is a house of cards, in his phrase, and he slept badly the night he admitted it.

Steve Chen

Steve Chen@calltoleap

How long could you survive if your income stopped tomorrow? I was making 270,000 a year and we had one month of runway. And my spouse at the time asked me a question that almost broke me.

Watch on TikTok (7,128 views, 244 likes, 15 comments, 39 shares). One month of runway on $270,000.

His answer was a discipline so simple it fits on a sticky note. Live on 70 percent, invest 20 percent, keep 10 percent in cash reserves, and stop measuring wealth by salary. Then he prices freedom with the classic multiple. Multiply annual expenses by 25 to find the invested sum that makes work optional, and fund it in a strict order with debt first, then savings buffers, then the retirement accounts and low cost index funds. Keep it simple is his refrain, and the engagement rate of 0.05 suggests the small audience passed it on.

"I stopped measuring wealth by my salary. I started a simple discipline. Live on 70 percent, invest 20 percent, keep 10 percent in cash reserves."

Humphrey Yang closes the circle by rebuilding from zero. In a September video watched 19,834 times with 1,069 likes, 45 comments and 72 shares, he lists four things he would do if he lost everything and started over. The framework is built for exactly the person Daniel's averages describe: someone mid journey realising the pot is thin and needing a sequence that works from any starting line.

Humphrey Yang

Humphrey Yang@humphreytalks

Four things that I would do if I had to start over financially. So if I lost all of my money and had to start from scratch, the first thing that I would do would be to take a look at all my expenses and make sure that they fit the following framework.

Watch on TikTok (19,834 views, 1,069 likes, 45 comments, 72 shares). The start over framework.

His framework runs needs at 55 percent of income, discretionary spending capped at 25 percent, and the rest saved and invested. Then three months of expenses into a high yield savings account, then a Roth IRA or the workplace match first where one exists, then income growth instead of ever tighter frugality. The last point is pure Steve and Erin. You can only cut so much, so make more and route the surplus to the same boring accounts. His tax free growth pitch for the retirement account lands in one sentence and sticks.

"Now number three I would definitely open a Roth IRA to start my investing journey. If my company offered a 4 0 1 k match, I would probably take that and prioritize that first before the Roth IRA."

Humphrey Yang on TikTok

Where they agree, and where they split

On the core claim there is no daylight. Daniel, Erin, Steve and Humphrey all treat the pension or investment balance as a multiple to be measured honestly and raised steadily, never as a vibe. Daniel says check the pot, then check it again. Erin says route side income straight into investing and debt payoff. Steve says live on 70 percent and multiply expenses by 25. Humphrey says fit spending to the framework and automate the surplus. Attention plus arithmetic beats intention every time.

"If you earn the £60,000, that would be £240,000 in your pension to aim for. So check your pot, then check it again."

Daniel on TikTok

The split opens on urgency versus sequence. Daniel and Steve press benchmarks with teeth, twice salary by 40, four times salary as the guideline, 25 times spending for freedom, because soft targets produce soft pots. Erin and Humphrey press sequence with patience, kill the debt first, build the buffer, then invest, because benchmarks without behaviour change scare people into paralysis. Steve bridges the two, pairing the strictest multiple on the page with the gentlest starting instruction, sell the closet clutter and save $100.

Check your pot against twice your salary this week, then write down the gap without flinching. Kill expensive debt first, hold three to six months of essentials in accessible savings, and route every spare pound or dollar into the pension or retirement account before lifestyle absorbs it. Erin proved at 27 that speed comes from side income, not salary. Steve proved at $270,000 that income without reserves is fragile. Daniel proved the average pot is £30,000 to £50,000, which means almost any sustained system puts you ahead. Start the system now and let compounding do the part you cannot.
