Overpay the Mortgage or Invest? What 6 Creators Say

Dahlia stages a 30 year contest between a £500 overpayment and a £500 investment, while five more creators argue about maths, peace of mind and property risk.

By SaveShare.io · 9 November 2026

Overpay the mortgage or invest the difference, told by six creators with numbers, from a 30 year thought experiment to a £425,000 London flat that lost £100,000

Back to Blog Mortgage Investing US UK

The scene is a thought experiment with two identical mortgages. Dahlia, the London creator behind Get On Top Money, imagines two borrowers with the same £300,000 loan at 4.5 percent across 30 years. One sends an extra £500 to the lender monthly, hungry for the peace of a paid off home. The other puts £500 into a tracker fund. Thirty years pass in two minutes, and the gap is enormous. The clip drew 580 plays with 26 likes and 10 comments, modest numbers for an enormous question.

The tension is one every homeowner with spare cash will recognise. On paper the contest looks settled: markets have historically returned more than mortgages charge. But a spreadsheet is not a life, and the overpayer buys something the market cannot sell, the certainty of owning the roof outright years early. Guaranteed saving against possible growing, peace of mind against probability, and both sides point to real numbers.

Dahlia

Dahlia@getontopwithdahlia

So I'm gonna overpay my mortgage by £500 every month. I'm gonna invest £500 a month into an s and P 500 ETF instead. It tracks the 500 biggest US companies and it's average 10% annual returns over the past 50 years.

Watch on TikTok (580 plays, 26 likes, 10 comments, 5 shares). The 30 year overpay versus invest contest.

Dahlia runs both lives to their conclusions, and the investor finishes roughly £300,000 ahead even after subtracting all mortgage interest paid. The overpayer saves £108,000 in interest and clears the debt 12 years early, then invests late with the freed payment. Too late: the early compounding years do the heaviest work.

"It's Just maths, really. My £500 a month has had more time to grow in the market at 10% while your mortgage overpayments were only giving you an effective return of 4.5%."

Dahlia on TikTok

If Dahlia supplies the maths, Amanda supplies the mercy. She posts daily about personal finance and noticed the scolding tone in the overpayment debate, the suggestion that anyone on a 1 or 2 percent fix who failed to overpay had wasted a golden window. Her answer is for everyone who lived through those years choosing between the mortgage and the supermarket, and her most watched recent video drew 144,752 plays with 2,145 likes and 523 comments.

Amanda

Amanda@amandainvests

It's completely normal to not be able to afford to overpay on your mortgage. Talking about my remortgaging, a lot of people have said that with such low rates, you know, 1 to 2%, everybody who had those rates should be overpaying. There's quite a few reasons why you might not have been overpaying.

Watch on TikTok (595 plays, 2,145 likes on her top recent video, 523 comments). Why normal people skipped overpayments.

Her reasons are free of judgment. Some households were surviving the cost of living crisis, and feeding the family beat trimming the mortgage. Others invested spare cash elsewhere for better returns, planning to clear a chunk at renewal. Plenty had stretched to buy at all, leaving nothing for the lender.

"So whatever your reason, if you are not overpaying on your mortgage, please don't feel bad about it. Look into overpayments and whether they would work For you."

Amanda on TikTok

Across the Atlantic, Becca Bergmann refuses to pick a team and offers a rule instead. Her 60 second framework sorts spare cash by the interest rate it would kill: above 10 percent, attack the debt, because cards and payday loans devour more than markets deliver. Between 7 and 10 percent, do both. Below 7 percent, favour investing. The explainer was watched 2,078 times with 149 likes and 12 comments.

Becca Bergmann

Becca Bergmann@beccanomics101

Should you pay off your debt or should you invest? Here's exactly how to figure out which is best for you in 60 seconds! If your highest interest rate debt is over 10% interest, so this would be like credit card debt, payday loans, some personal loans.

Watch on TikTok (2,078 plays, 149 likes, 12 comments, 5 shares). The interest rate rule for debt versus investing.

Becca adds the rule is a starting point, not a sentence. Low rate debt still deserves extra payments once retirement saving is on track, because the spreadsheet answer and the human answer differ. Her own behaviour, documented across many updates, leans toward the joy of killing the mortgage.

"If your highest interest rate is less than 7% interest, your priority should be investing because your investments are going to outpace the amount that you're being charged in interest. Now does this mean that you should never pay off low interest debt? No, not necessarily."

Becca Bergmann on TikTok

That leaning becomes a lifestyle in her payoff series, where she throws business income at the loan in public. One September update walks through $18,761 of income, subtracts living costs near $5,000, carves out tax and reinvestment, and fires the rest at the balance. The episode drew 10,145 plays with 855 likes and 144 comments, and that single payment wipes $17,000 of interest and 11 months off the term.

Yesterday I shared that my husband and I made $18,000 in September between our seven income streams. And unfortunately we don't get to keep all that money. So let me show you exactly how much we have left and we'll use it to make an extra payment to my mortgage.

What makes it compelling is the discipline underneath. Spending holds flat while income grows, so new revenue flows to the lender. She gives away a budgeting spreadsheet that 20,000 people use. It is Dahlia's overpayer brought to life, with seven income streams funding the payments.

"Just one extra payment will save me over $17,000 in interest and take 11 months off my mortgage. I've made almost $60,000 in extra payments so far this year, which is so cool and I'm so proud of us."

Becca Bergmann on TikTok

The counterweight is Jeremy Schneider, who thinks the debate starts too late. His rule concerns the day of purchase: never put down less than 20 percent, and if the deposit is not there, keep renting. Thin deposits buy fatter payments plus insurance protecting the lender at the borrower's expense. His warning drew 1,593 plays with 49 likes.

Jeremy Schneider

Jeremy Schneider@personalfinanceclub

Essentially, my first rule is I would never do less than 20%. Just rent. If you can't get a 20% down payment together, you are in the category of you're risking being house poor.

Watch on TikTok (1,593 plays, 49 likes, 4 comments, 4 shares). The 20 percent deposit rule.

Jeremy reframes the question from the other end. A buyer stretching in with minimum down has already chosen the most expensive loan before a single overpayment is discussed. Saving the full deposit first is, in his telling, the original overpayment, made before the mortgage exists.

"If you don't, you're gonna be on the hook for an even higher housing payment. So you have no money, you're gonna get an even higher housing payment."

Jeremy Schneider on TikTok

Back in Britain, Mark shows the balanced version inside a real budget. On a £112,500 Oxfordshire salary, he pays himself first with £300 into investments before any bill is touched. Then £2,500 flows to the joint account covering the mortgage, £675 to food, and the rest covers car, childcare and spending. His October episode drew 6,710 plays with 118 likes.

Mark

Mark@markonthemoney

I'm a big fan of paying yourself first. So the very first thing I do is load £300 into my trading 212 account for investments.

Watch on TikTok (6,710 plays, 118 likes, 23 comments, 10 shares). Paying himself first on payday.

Mark never frames it as mortgage versus markets. Investments fund automatically on payday, the mortgage is serviced from the joint account like any bill. Most households will not route every pound optimally, but a system funding both monthly beats a debate funding neither.

"After that I put £2,500 into our joint account for household bills to cover our mortgage and things like that. And another £675 goes in for our food and toiletries."

Mark on TikTok

Then Rex arrives with cold water: a Shepherd's Bush one bed bought for £425,000 in 2017 and sold for £325,000, a £100,000 loss before fees. With stamp duty, legal costs, eight years of payments and service charges, the owners fed roughly £150,000 in to walk away with £11,000. His retelling drew 14,650 plays with 304 likes and 72 shares.

Rex

Rex@theeverydaymillionaire

This London flat was sold for £100,000 less than it was bought for. So what can we learn from this? It's a one bed flat in Shepherd's Bush that they paid £425,000 for in 2017.

Watch on TikTok (14,650 plays, 304 likes, 46 comments, 72 shares). The flat that lost £100,000.

Rex draws three lessons, each complicating the overpayment instinct. A thin deposit leaves no safety net, since selling below the price with 5 percent down means owing money to leave. Early mortgage years are mostly interest, so overpaying into a falling asset means paying extra for the privilege of losing money. And prices do not always rise, with London flats down 4 percent while houses held up.

"Prices don't always go up. Especially flats. Flats in London went down 4%."

Rex on TikTok

Where they agree, and where they split

On the central maths there is no real disagreement. Dahlia, Becca and Mark accept that markets have historically outgrown the interest saved by overpaying a mid single digit mortgage, and nobody disputes the 10 percent against 4.5 percent shape of the contest. Amanda and Jeremy agree by implication, since neither urges raiding savings to feed the lender. The weight of voices sits with the market side, and the dissent is not about arithmetic.

"So whatever your reason, if you are not overpaying on your mortgage, please don't feel bad about it. Look into overpayments and whether they would work For you."

Amanda on TikTok

The split is about what the maths leaves out. Dahlia grants that investing wins on paper while overpaying buys guaranteed peace of mind, and Becca lives the contradiction, preaching the 7 percent rule while sprinting toward a paid off house. Amanda reminds borrowers that spare cash is a luxury many never had, while Rex warns that a mortgage is a bet on an asset that can fall. Jeremy would move the argument earlier, to the deposit, where the expensive mistakes are made. Same numbers, different anxieties, and the honest answer depends on which one keeps the reader up at night.

"It's Just maths, really. My £500 a month has had more time to grow in the market at 10% while your mortgage overpayments were only giving you an effective return of 4.5%."

Dahlia on TikTok

Check the rate first, since anything near double digits should be cleared before investing enters the conversation. Then fund both sides automatically, a fixed investment on payday and whatever overpayment the budget survives, inside wrappers that shelter the growth. Keep a deposit cushion thick enough that a falling market cannot trap anyone, and release guilt about years when surviving was the strategy. Peace of mind and probability are both worth buying, in proportions tuned to the household.
