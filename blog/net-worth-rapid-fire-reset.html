There are less than ninety days left in the year, and Trell the Trainer has no patience for anyone planning to start in January. Standing in front of his camera, he fires off thirteen things to do before the calendar turns, sorted into three categories, delivered at a pace that suggests he has watched too many people waste too many Decembers. Stop the bleeding, build the foundation, grow the money. The clip has been watched 98,011 times with 17,079 likes and 2,464 shares, which suggests the bluntness landed exactly where it was aimed.

The tension at the heart of this page is the one Trell names in his final rule and everyone else circles around. A single number is supposed to tell the truth about your finances, assets minus everything owed, updated honestly and often. But numbers invite comparison, and comparison invites either despair or complacency, depending on which side of someone else's screenshot you land on. These five creators all track the same scoreboard. They disagree about how loud the crowd should be while you check it, and about whether the number measures discipline, income, patience, or some uneasy mix of all three.

Trell opens with the least glamorous move first, and the ordering is deliberate. Before the investing accounts and the income skills and the scoreboard itself, there is the reckoning with the real monthly number. Not the estimate in your head, the statements pulled and added up, the figure that survives contact with the bank app. His own portfolio grew from $7,000 to almost $400,000 in about seven years because he invested every single month, and the sprint he prescribes is the compressed version of that habit, a ninety day burst where spending gets interrogated line by line and the freed cash goes straight into the market.

Trell the Trainer

Trell the Trainer@trellthetrainer

Number one. Figure out exactly what it cost you to live every single month. Not what you think you spend. Pull your exact bank statements, your exact credit card statements. You need to know the real number.

The middle of his list turns structural. Save a first $2,000 buffer, split income seventy twenty ten across living, investing and saving, automate everything, claim the retirement match, open the Roth and actually invest it. Each step removes one more decision. Then comes the line that splits this page in two. Saving has a ceiling and earning does not, aimed at the eight dollar subscription cancellers carrying eleven hundred dollar car notes.

Humphrey Yang approaches the same scoreboard from the opposite direction, not with a sprint but with an audit. Where Trell lists what to do, Humphrey lists what keeps people stuck, four mistakes that hold net worth below $100,000, ranked with the most common saved for last. His video drew 29,800 views with 1,255 likes and 96 comments, a comment count that suggests viewers recognised themselves in at least one of the four. The first mistake is the car, and the numbers he cites are worth sitting with. The average new car payment is now $770 a month, and with insurance, gas and maintenance the real cost drifts toward a thousand dollars or more. On a $65,000 salary that is roughly a quarter of take home pay rolling down the driveway every morning.

The second mistake is the shortcut economy, prediction markets and sports gambling dressed up as investing, which almost nobody wins over time. Then come the two quiet killers. Not knowing your savings rate hides every other mistake, and carrying a card balance at over 21 percent means losing money faster than Warren Buffett himself ever made it, about 20 percent a year. Read that comparison twice. Kumiko Love, known to millions as the Budget Mom, slows the whole conversation down to first principles. In lesson twenty eight of her thirty day Money School series, a video seen 3,681 times, she argues that if she could track only one number it would be net worth, not income, not the credit score. The reasoning is structural. Income can rise while debt rises faster. A checking balance can fall because money moved into retirement, which is progress wearing a disguise. The credit score measures borrowing behaviour, not wealth. Only the net worth statement shows both sides of the ledger at once.

Kumiko Love

Kumiko Love@thebudgetmom

If I can only track one number to measure my financial health, it would not be my income, it wouldn't be my credit score. It would actually be my net worth. And here's why. Because your net worth gives you one of the most accurate snapshots of your finances at any given point in time.

Watch on TikTok (3,681 views, Money School lesson 28). Assets on one side of the teeter totter, liabilities on the other.

Her teaching image is a teeter totter, assets on one side and liabilities on the other. When assets rise the liabilities side drops, which is the direction of travel, and the picture works at every scale, from three figures to seven. A teenager with no debt and a small balance has a healthy teeter totter. A high earner with larger debts has a heavier problem no salary screenshot can fix. Direction beats position, always.

Then the markets and the monthly transfers did their quiet work, and the number arrived. Where they agree, and where they split

The unanimous vote is for tracking the number itself. Trell calls it the scoreboard and makes it the thirteenth and final rule. Kumiko would keep it if she could keep only one figure. Humphrey builds his entire warning system around the distance to $100,000. Tatiana journaled the goal in January and watched it arrive. Georgia measures the small balances with the same seriousness others reserve for large ones. Nobody on this page improved a number they were not watching, and the monthly reckoning, statements pulled, balances compared, surplus routed, is the one ritual all five share across countries, incomes and audience sizes.

The split is the one Trell states outright. You cannot save your way to financial freedom, he says, there is a limit to cutting and no limit to earning. Humphrey stands closest to him, naming the car payment and the gambling habit as leaks no app can redeem. Georgia and Kumiko emphasise direction over velocity, the £500 milestone and the comparison against your own past, which reads as patience against his urgency. Tatiana sits in the middle, £85,000 saved by discipline and £33,000 added by the market.

The weight of the voices sits with a synthesis rather than a side. Income raises the ceiling, which is why Trell demands a new skill and Humphrey prices the car payment as a quarter of a salary, while systems and patience capture the surplus, which is why Kumiko preaches the teeter totter, Georgia preaches the cake, and Tatiana journaled a number and let compounding finish the job. Cut without earning and the number stalls. Earn without tracking and the surplus evaporates. Track without celebrating and the saver quits before the compounding starts. Do all three and the scoreboard moves every month, whether the balance has three figures or six.

The practical order writes itself. Pull the statements and learn the real number the way Trell demands. Kill the drag Humphrey names, the car payment, the bets, the unknown savings rate, the revolving balance. Set the teeter totter the way Kumiko teaches. Write the bold goal the way Tatiana did. Celebrate the £500 the way Georgia insists, because the saver who feels progress keeps making it.
