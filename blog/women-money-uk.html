The defining image of British money advice used to be a man in a suit explaining pensions on breakfast television. That picture is fading. On UK money TikTok, the loudest voices telling young women to open a stocks and shares ISA, automate their payday, and stop apologising for wanting wealth are women themselves, filming from bedrooms and flatshares in London and Manchester. The urgency is statistical. Fewer than a third of British women hold a stocks and shares ISA, and the investing gap widens with every year of compounding lost. Five creators are doing more than most high street banks to close it.

What makes them worth listening to is not glamour but method. None of the five sells a lottery ticket version of wealth. Their shared premise is that the gap is not a spell cast on women by the universe, but a systems problem with a systems solution. Pay yourself first, use boring tax wrappers, let time do the heavy lifting. The disagreement, and it is a friendly one, is about where the emphasis falls. Earn more or spend less. Rent or buy. Speak openly about money or keep your head down and automate. The videos below let them make each case in their own words.

Amy of Financially Her starts where most advisers are afraid to go, with earnings. A former banking employee who built six figures of investments by the age of 26, she argues that frugality has a ceiling and income does not. Her signature video on the subject, watched 1,963 times with 118 likes, distils the whole philosophy. Stop trying to shrink your life, she says, and start widening the gap between what you earn and what you spend, because that gap is where wealth is built.

Amy of Financially Her

Amy of Financially Her45.6K followers on TikTok

"I started my corporate career on around £30,000 and then two and a half years later, ended up on 85 5K. And this is what gave me considerably more money to actually work with. Cause there's a ceiling to how much you can cut back. But your earning potential is so much greater."

Amy also reframes the emergency fund as something more rebellious. "I quit my six figure job in banking without another job lined up and to do this, I spent years building a fuck you fund. Most people might call it an emergency fund, but I think that's a little bit too limiting to what it can do for you." She then shows the maths plainly. Work out the bare minimum monthly number, pick how many months of freedom to buy, and park the total somewhere accessible. Financial independence, in her telling, does not always mean retiring at 40. Sometimes it means being 26 and able to say no.

Kafilat speaks to the woman who earns well and still ends the month at zero. Her diagnosis is precise. The problem is rarely income and almost always infrastructure. In her Save Maxing series she walks through the familiar cycle. Payday optimism, the forgotten subscription, the quiet raid on savings, the guilt. Her third episode, on realistic saving targets, puts it plainly.

"You save consistently, but your savings isn't growing. Welcome back to Episode 3 of Save Maxing, where I go through how to build your savings successfully. So it's payday, you're feeling motivated, and you've moved £300 into your savings. But then week two hits and your account becomes overdrawn because you forgot a subscription was due to come out."

Where Amy preaches earning more, Kafilat preaches organising better. "Cutting out the coffees is actually having a system for your money. A lot of people just operate from one bank account, whereas you're meant to have multiple bank accounts." She then sorts accounts by purpose. A cash ISA for tax free interest, a high yield account for the buffer, and separate pots so bills never ambush savings. The two philosophies look opposed and are really sequential. Earn the gap, then give the gap somewhere to live.

DiDi turns the same lesson into communal maths. Filming as Deisha, a twenty something building wealth while refusing to stop travelling, she makes compound growth feel like a group project. Her most analytical video asks what it costs to retire a millionaire at different starting ages, and the figures have travelled. The video holds 13,162 views and 1,014 likes, and the conclusion does the persuading.

"At the age of 30, you would need to invest £535. At the age of 25, you would need to invest £350. And at the age of 18, you would need to invest just £190 every month. So are we seeing the pattern here?"

Watch on TikTok

The reason it travels is tone. DiDi refuses the hair shirt version of finance. "You love treating yourself, but you also want to build long term wealth. Then you've come to the right place." Her most viral savings video, a walkthrough of an account paying 6.5 percent that drew 57,455 views and 2,436 likes, works the same way. Small monthly deposits, explained plainly, with the interest calculated on screen. Saving £300 a month for a year to earn £125 in interest sounds almost too modest to matter, until thousands of viewers realise they could do it too.

Sophie Blank supplies the infrastructure underneath all this ambition. A German born tech strategist living in London, she documents a climb from minus £33,000 in student debt to more than £220,000 invested, powered by what she calls boring accounts. A pension, a stocks and shares ISA, a lifetime ISA, held for years. Her Wealth Codes episode on Hampstead, with 75,549 views and 5,140 likes, reads the city through money, but her most useful content is plumbing. Sinking funds.

"My sinking funds are the one reason I pay off my credit card every single month and I never go into debt while building my credit score and paying for things like summer holidays, Christmas presents and any other surprises. And here's why I think sinking funds are the most underused tool. If you're highly ambitious as well, you never panic spend on credit. The money is already there."

Sophie is also the most prominent renter in the group, and she owns the controversy. Paying £31,000 a year for a Zone 1 flat on a six figure salary, she argues the maths favours investing over buying, and a video on the subject drew 42,837 views. Her position, in her own words.

I have an audacious goal of becoming a millionaire by the time I'm 40, and I don't think owning a property will get me there."

Hannah Bevington is the youngest voice here and the most insistent that systems beat willpower. Investing for five years since her early twenties, she reached £135,000 by 26 and preaches a payday routine with four fixed moves. Check the payslip, clear high interest debt, sort money into pots, invest whatever remains. The first move sounds fussy until you hear why.

Hannah Bevington

Hannah BevingtonMoney creator on TikTok

"The really unfortunate thing about building wealth is it doesn't just happen. You really have to curate it and put certain systems in place to ensure you get there. So here are the four things that I do on payday every single month. The first thing is I always check I've been paid the right amount."

Watch on TikTok

Hannah is equally blunt about who should not invest yet. "You're putting high hopes on the fact that investing is going to get you out of that hole and within a few years you're going to be rich. It's not the case." The bracing honesty is popular. A video in which she crowdsources credit card advice drew 39,417 views and 1,780 likes, and her three step beginner framework, open a stocks and shares ISA, try a quarter of income in low cost index funds, hold for six to twelve years, is among the clearest on UK money TikTok. Her refrain is time in the market, not timing.

Put the five side by side and the agreements outweigh the rows. Amy and Hannah both refuse to demonise coffee, and Kafilat agrees, telling followers that clarity comes from systems rather than cutting small pleasures. Where they differ is the starting lever. Amy pulls income first, Kafilat pulls structure first, DiDi pulls community and consistency, Sophie pulls automation, Hannah pulls habit. None of them promises speed. Sophie shows eight years of statements to prove that compounding needs a long runway, and Hannah warns that the early months of investing will likely feel rocky.

The synthesis is reassuringly dull, which is the point. Open the ISA, automate the monthly purchase, keep the buffer in a high yield account, talk about money with friends. Sophie tracks her net worth against retirement scenarios on a monthly money date. Hannah invests whatever she can each month, sometimes £50 and sometimes thousands, protecting the habit over the amount. Kafilat would approve of the realism. "Saving £100 consistently is so much more better than saving £300 to then later in the month, pull out £200."

Visibility compounds too. Every video linked here shows its working, with figures stated by the creators themselves and open for anyone to check. The investing gap will not close because one video goes viral. It will close because thousands of women copy a payday routine, open the ISA, and leave the money alone for a decade. Start small, automate everything, and let time do what time does. That is the whole sermon, delivered five ways.
