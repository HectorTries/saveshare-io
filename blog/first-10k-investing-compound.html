Your First £10K: How Creators Turned Small Starts Into $400K and $3.3M

Three creators, three starting amounts, one shared argument, the size of your first deposit matters less than the date of it

By SaveShare.io · 25 October 2026

Three creators, three starting amounts, one shared argument, told with numbers

Back to Blog Investing Pension

Seven years ago Trell saved his first $10,000. He took $7,000 of it, opened a Roth IRA, bought an index fund and a couple of single stocks, and then did the hardest thing in investing, nothing. He did not check the account. He did not watch the market. When he finally looked, the balance read nearly $400,000, and his maths said that if he never invested another penny, compounding alone would carry the portfolio to $11 million by retirement. The video explaining this has been watched 435,976 times, with 63,240 likes and 15,399 shares, which makes it one of the most passed-around investing clips in our entire collection. People do not share it because the numbers are big. They share it because the starting number is small.

The tension underneath every story here is the same, and it is not really about stock picking. It is about whether ordinary people should wait until they feel ready to invest, until the debts are gone, the emergency fund is full, the salary is bigger, or whether waiting is itself the most expensive decision they will ever make. One of these creators turned $7,000 into $400,000 by starting early and sitting still. Another is 38 and on track for $3.3 million because she automated the boring steps. The third left university with a five-figure portfolio built £100 at a time. They disagree on accounts and assets, US Roths versus UK index funds, but they agree on the sentence that matters, start now, start small, then let time do the heavy lifting.

Trell frames the first $10,000 not as a milestone but as an escape velocity. His opening move is deliberately unglamorous, move everything out of the checking account earning a fraction of a percent and into a high yield savings account earning 4 to 5 percent, because inflation at 3 percent means idle money halves in buying power every 15 years. Step two is a credit card used exactly like a debit card, paid off monthly from the savings account, so everyday spending earns rewards instead of overdraft risk. Step three is the Roth IRA with a $250 monthly auto transfer. His own portfolio did the talking, $7,000 in, index funds plus a couple of growth bets, seven years of neglect, roughly $400,000 out.

Let me show you why it's so important to save your first $10,000. I just did the math on what my retirement portfolio would be worth at retirement if I just stopped investing today. If I never invested another penny into my investment portfolio. It's 11 million dollars. I just started investing six or seven years ago. I've been able to turn $7,000 into almost $400,000. Seven years ago, I saved my first $10,000. I took seven of that $10,000 and I opened a Roth IRA.

Watch on TikTok (435,976 views, 63,240 likes, 15,399 shares). Compound interest does the rest, he says, if you start early and stop fiddling.

The $11 million figure deserves a sceptical eyebrow, it assumes decades of high returns with no withdrawals, but the mechanism is real and the ordering is the lesson. High yield savings first, so cash stops rotting. Automated investing second, so behaviour stops mattering. Stock analysis never, because Trell's whole pitch is that checking your account is a hobby that costs money. Money is meant to be moved, he says, when money sits still, it dies.

Melissa Jean-Baptiste delivers the same message with less patience and more structure. She is 38, on track to retire with $3.3 million, and her opening line is a warning, you are overcomplicating the process. Her prescription is a three-step checklist designed to be completed in under 20 minutes. Sign up for the workplace 401k immediately at 3 percent, enough to feel it slightly, and raise it a point with every pay rise. Open an IRA at any mainstream brokerage, traditional or Roth depending on income, fund it, and then the step everyone skips, actually invest the money once it lands. An unfunded IRA, she says, is a shopping cart with no groceries in it.

Melissa Jean-Baptiste

Melissa@millennialindebt

I'm 38 years old and I am on track to retire with $3.3 million. I am also not afraid to hurt your feelings so listen to me when I tell you you're over complicating the process. You're going to start investing and you're going to start investing today. Step one, if your job offers a 401k you're going to sign up immediately. You're going to start with a 3% contribution. Your choice right now is 3% less today or $3 in retirement tomorrow. You choose.

Watch on TikTok (922 views, 176 likes). Step two is the IRA, step three is actually buying investments inside it.

What makes Melissa's version stick is the framing of the choice. Three percent less today or triple in retirement tomorrow. She also names the failure mode precisely, most beginners open the account, transfer the money, and leave it sitting in cash, earning nothing, technically invested in nothing. The account is the cart, the investments are the groceries, and an alarming number of people wheel an empty cart around for years. Her $3.3 million trajectory is not built on brilliance, it is built on refusing to leave the cart empty.

Janiice is the youngest voice on this page and possibly the most useful one for UK readers, because she built her start inside the constraints of student life. She left university with £10,000 in her investment portfolio, assembled £100 a month from a part time job plus whatever was left at month end, inside a Trading 212 account. Her rules were strict. Index funds and ETFs only, the boring vehicles with roughly 8 percent historical annual returns, no Palantir, no Nvidia, no single stock gambling until much later. And the rule that saved her the most money was not an investing rule at all, it was a spending rule, know what matters to you, buy only that, forget the trends.

Janiice

Janiice@janiice.myla

I left uni with £10,000 in my investment portfolio. Firstly, I would automate an amount that I would put into my Trading 212 account every month. I chose £100 cause I had a part time job at the time. But you could literally do it with any amount. You could start with £2, a fiver, a tenner. I would also really prioritize low risk stocks. At uni I only put my money into ETFs and index funds. Lastly, the thing that saved me the most was not giving into consumerism. Only spend your money on what you need and what you actually want to buy.

Watch on TikTok (34,930 views, 4,444 likes, 445 shares). Automation plus boring funds plus intentional spending.

Janiice's £2 minimum is the quiet radicalism of this page. Trell says save $10,000 first, Melissa says start with 3 percent today, Janiice says a fiver counts. All three converge on automation, money that moves itself before willpower gets a vote, and all three converge on boring assets early, index funds first, excitement later. Where Trell externalises growth to compounding time and Melissa to workplace defaults, Janiice internalises it to identity, deciding what kind of spender she is before deciding what kind of investor to be.

Where they agree, and where they split

On the core claim there is no daylight. Every creator here treats starting early with small amounts as strictly better than starting later with larger ones, because compounding rewards duration more than size. Trell's seven years turned $7,000 into $400,000. Melissa's decades of steady contributions project to $3.3 million. Janiice's £100 months compounded into a five-figure portfolio before graduation. The unanimous vote is that the best day to start was years ago and the second best day is today, preferably within the next 20 minutes.

The split opens on sequencing. Trell says build the $10,000 cash base first, then invest. Melissa says invest 3 percent from the very first paycheck, cash base or not. Janiice splits the difference, automate a tiny amount now and sweep the leftovers monthly. Underneath is a genuine disagreement about human nature, whether beginners need the safety of cash before they can tolerate market risk, or whether waiting for safety is just procrastination with a spreadsheet.

Nobody on this page timed the market. They automated, bought boring funds, and left the accounts alone. If there is one sentence all three would sign, it is Melissa's. You are overcomplicating the process.

The practical close is deliberately small, which is the point. Open the account this week, a stocks and shares ISA if you are in the UK, a Roth or 401k if you are in the US. Automate an amount you will not miss, £100, $250, a fiver, the number matters less than the direct debit. Buy a broad index fund, not a tip from the comments. Then do what Trell did, stop checking. Trell is sitting on $400,000 he barely looked at. Melissa is gliding toward $3.3 million on 3 percent increments. Janiice graduated with £10,000 and a habit. Different countries, different accounts, identical method, automate, buy boring, repeat.
