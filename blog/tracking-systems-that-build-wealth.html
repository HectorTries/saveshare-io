Apps, scoreboards and rules of thumb: the tracking systems behind six-figure net worths

Danny is twenty two, worth six figures, and keeps his financial life inside six phone apps. He used to track everything in a spreadsheet until the spreadsheet lost to a Notion template he built himself that more than five thousand strangers have downloaded. His tour of those apps pulled 162,410 views with 11,501 likes, which suggests the audience cares less about the balances and more about the machinery. How do you watch wealth happening.

The tension is about what deserves attention. Track too little and money leaks through rent and impulse buys. Track too much and the spreadsheet becomes a hobby that replaces saving. One creator here tracks six apps daily. Another tracks a single number and ignores the rest. They agree unmeasured money drifts. They disagree about which dial to stare at.

Danny is the systems maximalist, and his credibility is the number he opens with. Twenty two years old, financially responsible by his own description, already carrying a six figure net worth. His stack has a job for every dollar. Vanguard holds the Roth IRA and the brokerage. Marcus holds six months of expenses plus sinking funds for travel and gifts. Chase handles paychecks and travel points. Bilt turns rent into points with no annual fee. Experian watches the credit score. And Notion sits above all of it as the dashboard.

Danny tracked everything in Excel for years, then rebuilt the apparatus in Notion with a template over five thousand people have downloaded. He also runs a low buy year, documented in a video watched more than 2.6 million times, where intentional shopping means quality over quantity. The apps track the money and the low buy rules guard the exits.

Mia Rose McGrath tracks almost the opposite way. Where Danny watches accounts, Mia watches milestones. She is twenty five, describes herself as holding a multiple six figure net worth, and spent her early twenties raising income and cutting expenses toward financial freedom. Her most cited video lays out four milestones for the whole decade, watched 71,024 times with 5,501 likes, and reads less like a budget than a syllabus.

Mia Rose McGrath

Mia Rose McGrath@miarosemcgrath

These are four financial milestones I think you should be trying to hit in your 20s. This is coming from a 25 year old that has a multiple six figure net worth. And I spent my early 20s increasing my income and reducing my expenses, all with the eventual goal of getting to financial freedom.

Watch on TikTok (71,024 views, 5,501 likes, 20 comments). Four milestones: emergency fund, no high interest debt, open a stocks and shares ISA, reach a 20 percent savings rate.

The four milestones are plain. Three months of expenses saved, longer if self employed. High interest debt cleared first, attacked by avalanche or snowball. A stocks and shares ISA opened with fifty or a hundred pounds, pointed at boring global index funds. And a twenty percent savings rate. The savings rate is her scoreboard, not the balance, just the ratio, and compounding does the rest.

Trell the Trainer tracks in steps, five of them, ordered like a checklist taped to the fridge. His baby steps video drew 129,854 views with 15,513 likes and 303 comments, and its authority rests on the number he repeats everywhere. Seven thousand dollars turned into more than four hundred thousand in under seven years, mostly by putting money in every single month. The steps are the system that produced the score.

Trell the Trainer

Trell the Trainer@trellthetrainer

Baby Step 2. Save your first $2,000 in a high yield savings account. We call this the Emergency Fund because most Americans are one emergency away from homelessness. the goal is to save at least three months of expenses in this account.

Watch on TikTok (129,854 views, 15,513 likes, 303 comments). Five baby steps from paycheck to paycheck to invested.

The full sequence runs debt first, highest interest first, paid biweekly because interest accrues daily. Then the emergency fund in an account paying at least three and a half percent, with direct deposit routed there instead of checking. Then a credit card treated exactly like a debit card, paid in full monthly, harvesting rewards on spending that would happen anyway. Then fifteen percent of income into a Roth IRA, where two hundred dollars a month from age twenty four becomes roughly one point two million tax free at retirement. Then a brokerage account fed automatically at two or three hundred dollars a month.

Automation is his tracker. Once the transfers run themselves, the balance becomes the receipt rather than the project. Trell turned $7,000 into over $400,000 in under seven years by investing every single month, and his projection says the account would still read eleven million at retirement even if he never contributed another dollar.

The Budget Mom would call all of this instrumentation noise. Her Money School series teaches one lesson a day in under two minutes, and in lesson twenty eight she makes the minimalist case. If she could track only one number, it would not be income, not the credit score, but net worth. The video is small, a few thousand views, but the argument is the load bearing wall of this entire post.

The Budget Mom

The Budget Mom@thebudgetmom

if I can only track one number to measure my financial health, it would not be my income, it would not be my credit score. It would actually be my net worth. And here is why. Because your net worth gives you one of the most accurate snapshots of your finances at any given point in time.

Watch on TikTok. Lesson 28 of a 30 day Money School series: net worth as a teeter totter of assets and liabilities.

When assets rise the board tips the right way, and when liabilities rise it tips back. Income cannot tell you whether wealth is building, she argues, because a raise paired with new debt is motion without progress.

Every other creator on this page publishes a number. The Budget Mom grants the scoreboard and forbids the leaderboard. Track yourself against yourself, month over month, and the direction is the verdict.

Humphrey tracks the leak before it happens, which is rent. His affordability breakdown reached 346,410 views with 11,862 likes and 271 comments, and its hook is that the standard rule is wrong. The old advice says rent should be thirty percent of gross salary. Humphrey says nobody pays rent from gross salary, so the rule should be thirty five percent of take home pay instead.

Humphrey

Humphrey@humphreytalks

So the old rule says that rent should be 30% of your gross salary. So $3,000 a month in rent means you need $120,000 per year. But that is outdated because you do not pay your rent out of your gross salary, you pay it out of your paycheck after taxes.

Watch on TikTok (346,410 views, 11,862 likes, 271 comments). Why the 30 percent rule understates what rent really costs.

The correction bites harder as rents rise. At six thousand dollars a month, the old rule asks for about two hundred and forty thousand a year, while his version asks for around two hundred and ninety six thousand. Nearly sixty thousand dollars of difference hiding inside a rule of thumb. His wider guardrail is that rent plus transport plus insurance plus recurring costs should sit under fifty to fifty five percent of total income. Track the biggest outflow first and the smaller ones mostly behave.

His second video, watched 239,891 times with 13,607 likes, names the milestone where tracking gets fun. At two hundred and fifty thousand invested, a two percent market day pays five thousand dollars. Reach it by thirty five and never invest again, and it still grows to roughly two and a half million by sixty five.

Lupes closes the loop by tracking the engine itself. Her investing videos are unambiguous. She invests in ETFs, she will not stop talking about ETFs, and if she ever stops buying ETFs it means she has stopped investing entirely. Her beginner guidance video drew 96,646 views with 10,066 likes, and her method is aggressively simple. Seven holdings inside a stocks and shares ISA, one global ETF at the core, checked the way a gardener checks soil.

I will never stop raving about the benefits of ETF investing. In fact, if I am no longer investing in etfs, it means I am no longer investing period. Lately I have been seeing a shift in the investing community where everybody is heading towards an individual stock portfolio.

She concedes the temptation honestly, since individual stocks spike in ways no ETF will ever print, and she holds some herself. But one ISA position opened in March was up eleven percent by August. For beginners she prescribes one global tracker inside the tax shelter, bought monthly and left alone.

Where they agree, and where they split

On the core claim there is no daylight. Every creator here treats measurement as the precondition for progress. Danny logs accounts in Notion, Mia computes a savings rate, Trell checks off baby steps, the Budget Mom watches net worth, Humphrey audits rent, Lupes reads the ISA statement. Different dials, identical belief.

The split opens on how many dials to watch. Danny says more is more, six apps plus a dashboard. The Budget Mom says one is enough, net worth alone, because every other number is an input or a distraction. Mia sits between them with four milestones per decade. Trell automates the watching away with transfers he never touches. Humphrey narrows the lens to rent, the largest leak. Lupes narrows it to the portfolio statement. The weight of voices sits with starting simple and adding detail only when the basics hold.

Nobody here defends ignorance as bliss. The closest thing to a dissent is Trell's, and even he tracks, just once at setup and then never again. If one sentence unites all six, it is the Budget Mom's. Compare the number to your own past self, watch the direction, and let the trend deliver the verdict.

The practical close is straightforward, which is rather the point. Pick one number to watch first, net worth or savings rate or rent share, and compute it this week from real statements. Put the emergency fund somewhere it earns interest and route pay there automatically. Open the tax sheltered account and buy the boring global fund every month without fail. Then check the scoreboard monthly, compare it only to your own past, and let compounding turn the early discipline into the later millions. Six creators, six dashboards, one direction, watch the money and it starts behaving.
