Credit cards, Klarna and student loans, what to clear first and why

← Blog Debt

Debt is the only topic on this site where the creators sound angry. They joke about market dips and shrug at bad months, but mention a credit card balance carried at 20 percent and the tone changes completely. Something personal has happened to each of them, a Tesco card maxed at 21, a student loan that dragged for years, a sinking fund system built precisely so it never happens again. The consensus is total and unusually emotional. Expensive debt dies before investing begins, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling something.

The most complete system belongs to Sophie Blank, who describes going from minus 33,000 pounds in student debt to multiple six figures saved and invested, and credits a single mechanism for keeping her out of debt for years while spending freely on holidays and Christmas.

Sophie Blank

Sophie Blank@itssophieblank

One of my biggest flexes in life is the fact that I have never had credit card debt, and the fact that I do not feel guilty about spending, ever. It is all thanks to sinking funds. They are the one system that has kept me out of debt for years, even while spending freely on holidays, buying Christmas presents, and paying for life surprises.

Watch on TikTok (2,007 views, 78 likes). Her sinking funds video.

Sophie started investing with 100 pounds in July 2018 with zero knowledge, paid off her student loan, and watched her net worth skyrocket afterward. Her own telling makes the sequence explicit. The loan was a financial drag for years, freedom came the month it cleared, and the sinking funds exist so she never slides back. Pots of money set aside beforehand for events and wishlist items, funded monthly, so the credit card never has to bridge a gap that planning already closed.

JNO states the mathematics with the bluntness of a man who ran the numbers before risking a pound. Opening his Investing from Zero series, he puts debt clearance ahead of everything, ahead of the emergency fund, ahead of the first investment, because the comparison is not close.

JNO

JNO@jno_pwnr

The first thing I did was get rid of any high interest debt, so things like credit cards. Because if you are paying 20 percent interest on a credit card, you would be much better off paying that off instead of investing in something that is going to go up or down.

Watch on TikTok (1,592 views). Steps two and three were a six month emergency fund and learning the basics first.

There is no hedging in that sentence and none is needed. A guaranteed 20 percent return from clearing a card beats any plausible market outcome, and the market outcome is not even guaranteed. JNO treats this as the entrance exam for investing. Pass it, build the buffer, learn the basics, and only then does a pound go near a share.

Daniel supplies the cautionary tale, told against himself. Looking back at 21, he names the single decision he would undo, a store card with a limit that now reads like a trap designed specifically for young men with new jobs.

Daniel

Daniel@gray.area.finance

If I could go back and speak to my 21 year old self, I would first of all ask him to lose some weight and keep it off. But other than that, here is the money advice I would give. Number one is do not get that Tesco credit card. They are going to give you a 6,400 pound limit and you are going to spend every single penny of it.

Watch on TikTok (2,151 views). A 31 year old father comparing his finances to UK medians in his most watched video, seen 80,400 times.

Daniel now makes measured videos about cash ISA allowances and the state pension triple lock, but the Tesco card story is the one his viewers remember, because most of them own its modern equivalent. The limit felt like wealth at 21. It was, as he eventually learned, a high interest loan wearing a loyalty scheme as a disguise.

Mia Rose widens the frame beyond cards to the milestones that make debt clearance automatic. In a video watched more than 71,000 times, she lists the financial targets she believes every twenty something should chase, and killing expensive borrowing sits at the centre of a broader choreography of saving, investing, and pension building.

Mia Rose

Mia Rose@miarosemcgrath

These are four financial milestones I think you should be trying to hit in your 20s. This is coming from a 25 year old that has a multiple six figure net worth. And I spent my early 20s in a very intentional way, building the habits that would carry me, paying off anything expensive I owed, saving my first emergency buffer, and then investing every single month without fail.

Watch on TikTok (71,024 views, 5,501 likes). One of the most liked videos in her catalogue, with 219 shares.

Mia never worked in finance and did not grow up financially literate, which is precisely why her milestones land. She spent seven years reading everything before trusting herself, and the order she teaches is the order she lived. Expensive debt cleared, buffer saved, monthly investing automated, and the compounding that followed did the rest.

Then Lupes walks in and complicates the sermon, deliberately. In one of his most loved clips he argues that zero percent finance, used by someone disciplined, is not debt at all but free money, and he would take it over cash almost every time.

Lupes

Lupes@lupeslively

Zero percent finance is the best thing since cooked food. To the point where I will even say if you got the option to buy something expensive in cash today or get it through zero percent finance, zero percent finance is almost always the better option. Almost, almost always. Because if you are not good with money, please do not finance anything. But if you are, then it just makes so much more sense.

Watch on TikTok (83,500 views). His caveat is doing heavy lifting: knowing that one pound today is worth more than one pound tomorrow only helps if the balance clears on time.

This is where the creators split, and the split is genuinely instructive. Sophie, JNO, Daniel, and Mia treat consumer borrowing as the enemy, full stop, because they have seen what it does to people who cannot clear it monthly. Lupes agrees about anything charging interest, then carves out a precise exception for interest free credit used by people with systems, arguing that keeping cash invested while paying over 24 months at zero percent is simple arithmetic. The weight of voices sits firmly with the absolutists, and Lupes himself concedes the point with his caveat. If you are not good with money, do not finance anything. Most people, most of the time, are safer acting as though he never made the exception.

The takeaway survives the disagreement intact. List every balance charging interest, attack the highest rate first while paying minimums elsewhere, automate sinking funds for the predictable lumps like Christmas and car repairs so new debt never starts, and only then begin investing. Sophie cleared 33,000 pounds of student drag and built six figures. Daniel still winces at a supermarket card from a decade ago. The market returns seven percent in a good year. Your card charges twenty in every year. Pay the card.

Hannah Bevington offers the counterweight, a creator deep in the credit card rewards game, asking her audience which card beats her Amex Gold setup. In a video watched nearly 40,000 times she crowdsources the best card perks, revealing in passing that her own spending runs through Amex for rewards.

Hannah Bevington

Hannah Bevington@hannahbevington

Quick question, what credit cards are we all using at the minute? I had the American Express British Airways card. I just found I was not using the flights and the companion voucher because I live in Manchester and a lot of the BA flights were flying from Heathrow. Which is why recently I switched to the Amex Gold card. I had accrued like 800 pounds worth of online shopping money which was great.

Watch on TikTok (39,417 views, 1,780 likes). She debates the 200 pound annual fee on camera and asks viewers what they use.

The contrast with Daniel could not be sharper, and it proves the rule rather than breaking it. Hannah accrued 800 pounds of shopping credit on her Amex Gold, while openly questioning whether the 200 pound annual fee is worth renewing. Daniel paid years of interest on a store card because he spent a 6,400 pound limit he could not clear. Same plastic, opposite outcomes, separated entirely by whether the balance is cleared monthly. Every creator on this page agrees on which side of that line to stand, and Hannah, the rewards optimiser among them, hunts perks while Daniel warns what happens when balances are not cleared.
