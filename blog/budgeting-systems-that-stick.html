Budgeting Systems That Stick: Low-Buy Years, Meal-Deal Maths and the 90-Day Reset

A low-buy year, a meal-deal affordability rule, a 90-day spending reset and milestone targets, told with numbers

Danny finished his 2024 low-buy year, declared it extremely successful, and promptly signed up to do it even bigger in 2025. The video announcing the resolution was watched 2,631,900 times, which makes it one of the most viewed budgeting clips in this entire collection, and the premise is almost offensively simple. A low-buy year does not mean no fun, it just means smarter spending. No banned categories handed down from a guru, no spreadsheet with forty tabs, just a personal rule about intentional shopping, quality over quantity, and a list of things bought as needed so impulse spending never gets a look in.

The tension underneath every story on this page is the same, and it explains why most budgets die by February. A budget has to do two jobs at once. It has to constrain spending enough to matter, and it has to feel livable enough to survive. Push too hard and the budget becomes a crash diet that ends in a binge. Stay too loose and it is just a diary of money leaving. The five creators here all found a different answer to that tradeoff, and their answers range from a year long shopping pact to a mathematical test for whether a sandwich is affordable.

Danny is the anchor because his system is the most complete. A financially responsible 22 year old with a six figure net worth, by his own accounting, he runs his money through a stack of apps with distinct jobs. His most watched budgeting video lays out the low-buy philosophy, while the follow up, seen 162,410 times with 11,501 likes, names the actual toolkit.

Danny

Danny@dimeswithdanny

Here are my favourite money apps as a financially responsible 22 year old with a 6 figure net worth okay starting off strong we have Vanguard this is what I used to invest with I have both my Roth IRA and my regular brokerage account here absolutely love them. Vanguard has in my opinion the best app it is easy to use easy to understand I literally taught my dad how to use it last week and he has not asked for help since which is a good sign.

Watch on TikTok (162,410 views, 11,501 likes, 66 comments). He keeps six months of expenses in a high yield savings account and runs his budget through a Notion template downloaded over 5,000 times.

The rest of the stack is just as deliberate. A high yield savings account holds six months of expenses plus sinking funds for travel and gifts. A Notion budgeting template, his own creation, replaced the Excel sheets he used for years. A travel credit card and a rent paying card handle spending, and a credit monitoring app keeps watch over the score. What makes Danny interesting is not any single app but the architecture, every dollar has a named home before the month begins, which is the same instinct behind the low-buy year, decide the rules once, then let the system carry the weight that willpower cannot.

Mia Rose McGrath gives the system its targets. A 25 year old with a multiple six figure net worth, she filmed a video laying out four financial milestones for your twenties, and it was watched 71,024 times with 5,501 likes. Where Danny describes the machinery, Mia describes the destinations the machinery should reach.

Mia Rose McGrath

Mia Rose McGrath@miarosemcgrath

These are four financial milestones I think you should be trying to hit in your 20s. This is coming from a 25 year old that has a multiple six figure net worth. And I spent my early 20s increasing my income and reducing my expenses, all with the eventual goal of getting to financial freedom. Number one is have 3 months worth of living expenses saved.

Watch on TikTok (71,024 views, 5,501 likes, 20 comments). Her other three milestones are killing high interest debt, opening a stocks and shares ISA, and reaching a 20 percent savings rate.

Her sequencing matters. Emergency fund first, then high interest debt like credit cards and Klarna, then investing through a stocks and shares ISA starting with fifty or a hundred pounds, then a 20 percent savings rate powered by compound interest. It is a budget expressed as a ladder rather than a ledger, and she is explicit that UK student loans sit outside the debt emergency because of how differently they work. The tone is older sister rather than lecturer, a woman who spent seven years teaching herself finance and now hands over the syllabus.

Gabriel Nussbaum brings the single funniest budgeting test on this page. In a 47 second video, he asks what salary you need to buy a Tesco meal deal every day at work without it hurting, and the answer shocks the comments. The clip pulled 79,234 views, remarkable for under a minute of arithmetic about a sandwich.

Gabriel Nussbaum

Gabriel Nussbaum@gabriel.nussbaum

A finance rule of thumb when it comes to budgeting your money is the 50 30 20 rule. 50% should be going towards your needs. Things like your rent, your bills. 20% towards your savings, your investments, your pensions. And 30% down the middle is towards your wants.

Watch on TikTok (79,234 views). His verdict is that a daily 3.85 pound meal deal needs just under 2,600 pounds of monthly take home pay, roughly a 38,000 pound salary.

The mechanism is what makes it stick. For any small cost to be negligible, he argues, it should be no more than 10 percent of your wants budget, which is 3 percent of total salary. A meal deal five days a week runs 77 pounds a month and 924 a year, which means the daily habit quietly demands a 38,000 pound salary to stay painless. It is the 50/30/20 rule weaponised into a single vivid example, and it reframes every small daily purchase as a salary question rather than a price question.

Trell is the loudest voice here and the least patient with smallness. In a video watched 133,789 times with 21,105 likes, he lays out exact steps to stop living paycheck to paycheck, and his opening move is a 90 day spending freeze, no unnecessary shopping, no takeaways, no trips. Then he picks a fight with the entire budgeting genre.

Trell

Trell@trellthetrainer

First thing we are gonna do is cut spending for just 90 days. No unnecessary shopping, no unnecessary Doordash eating out. Can not take any trips. The first place I would look is if I have a car note. 750 dollars or more. That is 10,000 dollars a year before insurance, gas, maintenance.

Watch on TikTok (133,789 views, 21,105 likes, 172 comments). His punchline is that the car payment, not the coffee, is what kills budgets.

His punchline lands hard. All these finance people telling you to stop getting coffee and cut your Netflix, that is not what is killing you, he says, it is the car note that is killing you. A 750 dollar monthly payment is 10,000 dollars a year before insurance, fuel and maintenance, so his advice is to halve the car rather than cancel the streaming. After the 90 day cut comes a high yield savings account paying at least 4 percent, then a high income skill, then index funds. It is budgeting as triage, stop the biggest bleed first, and his own exhibit is a 7,000 dollar portfolio he says he grew past 400,000 dollars.

Jenny Park closes the circle by outsourcing the whole problem. In a video watched 94,536 times, she describes handing her budgets to a team of certified financial planners who reviewed them line by line and rebuilt her timelines around her goals, buying a first home, staying on track for retirement, and getting reassurance she was doing things right.

Jenny Park

Jenny Park@mohaewithjennypark

They looked at my retirement and made some changes. They looked at my budgets and gave me feedback, and they also just created new timelines for me based on all the goals I had for my future. I have genuinely recommended them to all my friends even before I started working with them in an official capacity.

Watch on TikTok (94,536 views). She credits the planners with her 120,000 dollar debt payoff and first California home purchase.

Jenny is the only creator here who admits she worried about money alone and decided to stop. The planners walked her through selling company stock step by step, adjusted her retirement, and revised the roadmap whenever life changed. It is the premium version of Danny's app stack, a human system instead of a software one, and the result she reports is the largest headline number on this page, 120,000 dollars of debt cleared on the way to a first home.

Where they agree, and where they split

On the core claim there is no daylight. All five treat budgeting as a system you design once and then follow, not a decision you remake every time you open your wallet. Danny precommits with a low-buy year and named accounts. Mia precommits with milestones in a fixed order. Gabriel precommits with percentage rules that turn every purchase into a formula. Trell precommits with a 90 day freeze. Jenny precommits by handing the design to professionals. The unanimous vote is that structure beats willpower, and every one of them has numbers to show for it, from Danny's six figures at 22 to Mia's multiple six figures at 25 to Jenny's six figure payoff.

The split opens on small versus big. Gabriel and Danny sweat the small stuff deliberately, tracking meal deals and impulse buys because small leaks sink young budgets. Trell calls that entire approach a distraction, arguing the car payment dwarfs the coffee and that sweat belongs on income and investing instead. Mia sits between them, her milestones demand both expense discipline and income growth in the same breath. Jenny sidesteps the debate by paying someone else to referee it. Where the weight of voices sits, it sits with the small stuff first, three of the five built their systems around daily spending control, but Trell's dissent is the useful grit in the oyster, a reminder that no amount of sandwich maths fixes a car you cannot afford.

The practical close is straightforward. Give every pound or dollar a named home before the month starts, whether that means Danny's app stack, Mia's milestone ladder, or Jenny's planner. Test small daily habits against your income the way Gabriel tests the meal deal, and attack the biggest fixed cost the way Trell attacks the car note. Danny is into year two of buying less on purpose. Mia's milestones compound from age 25. Gabriel's sandwich costs 924 pounds a year. Different systems, identical insight, decide once, then stop deciding.
