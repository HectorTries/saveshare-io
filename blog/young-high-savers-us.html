Young High Savers: How US Creators Built Six Figures Before 30

At 22, Danny Penev sat down to film a tour of his phone, and the apps on it told a stranger story than most retirement seminars. Vanguard for the Roth IRA and brokerage, Marcus by Goldman Sachs for six months of emergency expenses, Notion for a budgeting template 5,000 people had downloaded, Chase for the paychecks, BILT for rent points, Experian for the credit score. Six figures of net worth, organised into folders, narrated by someone barely old enough to rent a car. The video is calm, specific and faintly absurd, which is exactly why it works. This is what compounding looks like when it starts before the wrinkles.

The uncomfortable question behind these stories is whether starting early matters more than earning big, and the creators here answer it from wildly different income levels. One maxed a Roth on a corporate salary while side hustles cleared debt. One stacked income streams until the average month dwarfed the baseline. One treats saving as a game of apps and hacks. They share an age bracket and almost nothing else, which makes their convergence on the same few principles worth examining.

Danny Penev is the systems maximalist, and his money apps video is the clearest statement of the philosophy. Every dollar has a venue, investing at Vanguard, emergencies at Marcus, spending through points earning cards, all of it tracked in a Notion template he built himself. His most watched video ever, seen 18,302,019 times, suggests the audience is less interested in any single app than in the spectacle of a 22 year old with a six figure net worth explaining his stack like an IT administrator.

Danny Penev@dimeswithdanny

Here are my favourite money apps as a financially responsible 22 year old with a 6 figure net worth. Okay starting off strong we have Vanguard. This is what I used to invest with. I have both my Roth IRA and my regular brokerage account here. Absolutely love them. Vanguard has in my opinion the best app. It is easy to use, easy to understand. I literally taught my dad how to use it last week and he has not asked for help since which is a good sign.

Watch on TikTok (162,410 views). Six months of expenses sit in Marcus, his budgeting template lives in Notion, and BILT earns points on rent with no annual fee.

The companion habit is the low buy year, which Danny ran through 2024 and resolved to beat in 2025. Not a no buy year but intentional shopping, quality over quantity, lists before purchases. The discipline targets slow leaks, the carts and impulse orders that compound in reverse.

Erin Confortini earned $71,000 in side hustles in her first calendar year at 22, freelancing and content and digital spreadsheets, and her allocation of it is the most instructive money decision on this page. After taxes and expenses left around $46,000, she aimed the entire sum at debt, because her $705 in monthly student loan and car payments were the obstacle between her and everything else, the solo apartment, the future career bets, the option to quit corporate life.

Erin Confortini@itserinconfortini

When I was 22, I really wanted to make money outside of my corporate job. And that first calendar year, I made $71,000 between all of my different side hustles. So here is what I did with all of my side hustle money. Obviously, you have to factor in taxes and business expenses, so that left me with around $46,000. My goal for my side hustle in that first year was primarily just paying off my debt.

Watch on TikTok (16,431 views, 1,363 likes). Corporate salary covered the Roth IRA, side hustle money bought freedom, debt free in 18 months.

Her corporate salary already covered investing, so the side hustle income had one job, erasing $705 in monthly overhead. Within 18 months the debt was gone and her cost of living had dropped permanently. Erin's later work generalises it for variable earners. Baseline expenses to your worst month, aim the average at goals, send peaks into skills.

His catalogue is a library of small recoveries, a Costco Next trick that prices a $620 Briggs and Riley case against its $860 retail twin, a Delta timer hack worth 2,500 miles for a 22 minute bag delay, an Amex cell phone protection claim that covered a $400 screen replacement for a $50 deductible. His Costco video drew 228,635 views with 18,469 likes, and his Delta hack nearly 278,000.

The underlying claim is serious. Wealth leaks through unclaimed benefits, protections on cards people hold, guarantees in contracts nobody reads. His household income content references $128,000, comfortable rather than stratospheric. These are not lottery tickets but maintenance work, performed with props and punchlines.

Trell the Trainer brings the intensity, and his numbers dwarf the others. Starting six or seven years ago with $7,000 from personal training work, he put 70 percent of his income into the market and watched it approach half a million dollars, projecting $11 million at retirement if he never invests another cent. His top five tips video drew 335,040 views, but the investment math video, 435,976 views with 63,240 likes and 15,399 shares, is the one that matters.

Trell the Trainer@trellthetrainer

I took my first $7,000 that I made from being a trainer and put it in the stock market. It is almost half a million dollars today. Not because one stock made me rich. I have consistently put 70 percent of my income into the stock market for the last six to seven years. You have to get to a place where you stop working for money and you make money while you sleep. Cause if you do not, you will work until you die.

The 70 percent rate is the part nobody can copy without wincing, which is why his first tip is six months of disappearance. No dating, no birthdays, no trips, stack everything. The method is extreme, the maths is ordinary, time plus contributions plus patience at emergency intensity.

Becca Bergmann shows what the surplus years compound into when the household is the unit of analysis. At 31, she and her husband run seven income streams, high yield savings interest at 4.5 percent, affiliate links at $375 a month, video revenue at $706, a paid off starter house now rented out, digital products, and more, tracked monthly smallest to largest with millionaire in their thirties as the stated trajectory.

Becca Bergmann@beccanomics101

I am 31 years old and my husband and I have seven income streams. I wanna break down exactly how much money came from each last month. We are on track to be millionaires in our 30s. And increasing our income and diversifying our income streams is quite literally the only reason that is possible. Every month I get paid interest for keeping my emergency fund in our high yield savings account and get about $100 a month from that.

Watch on TikTok (14,563 views). The rental is a paid off starter home, the emergency fund earns at SoFi, affiliates are the growth focus.

Becca's breakdown is notable for its honesty about scale. The rental made $11 that month, a rounding error, because the story is the paid off asset rather than the cash flow. The emergency fund earns $100 monthly just for existing at 4.5 percent. Diversification here does not mean seven large streams, it means seven streams at different maturities, some compounding quietly while others are actively grown. It is the household version of Erin's baseline and average logic, extended across a decade, and the destination she names, millionaires in their thirties, is presented as arithmetic rather than aspiration.

Where they agree, and where they split

The unanimous vote is for earliness and automation. Danny's Roth at 22, Erin's monthly maxing from her corporate salary, Trell's $250 automatic Roth transfer, Becca's emergency fund earning while she sleeps, every one of them removed the savings decision from the monthly calendar and let time do the heavy lifting. John is the partial exception, his hacks are manual by nature, but even he automates the cell phone bill through the card specifically to trigger the protection. Set it once, benefit for years, that sentence could be the shared motto.

The split is on intensity versus sustainability, and it is stark. Trell prescribes disappearance, 70 percent savings rates, six months of social hibernation, the top 1 percent framing worn proudly. Danny's low buy year is Trell's monasticism made livable, restraint with room for joy, quality over quantity rather than nothing at all. Erin lands between them, aggressive allocation of windfalls paired with a budgeting system designed for irregular income rather than against it. John opts out of the intensity debate entirely, arguing that the marginal dollar is better found in unclaimed benefits than in skipped lattes. Becca's seven streams imply the long run answer is neither frugality nor hacks but income architecture, more streams maturing over more years.

The weight of the evidence favours the boring middle. Trell's results are real but his inputs are unavailable to most people, while Danny's app stack, Erin's debt sequencing and John's benefit recovery are all executable this week. Becca's trajectory suggests the endpoint of doing the boring things for a decade is the seven stream household, which reframes the whole debate. Intensity buys speed, systems buy permanence, and permanence wins because it survives the years when intensity is impossible.

The practical close writes itself in order of operations. Open the Roth and automate a monthly contribution, however small, because every creator here started there or wishes they had. Route an emergency buffer into high yield savings so the next shock does not touch investments. Aim windfalls and side income at the highest cost overhead first, the way Erin erased $705 a month. Audit the benefits already attached to cards and memberships, the way John does, because free money is the easiest money. Then keep the system running while life happens, since the six figure 22 year old and the seven stream 31 year old differ only in how many years the machine has been on.
